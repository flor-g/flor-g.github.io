# 0. Introduction: three questions and the level at which they are asked

Although *some* is compatible with *all*, a listener who hears *some* often understands *some but not all*. The question is how the listener arrives at *some but not all* when the lexical meaning permits *all*. In this paper, *scalar resolution* names the selection of a value on a scale, and *scalar implicature* names the pattern in which the interpretation of *some* falls outside the region associated with *all* (§4.1.2). *Scalar strengthening* is a proposed mechanism for producing this pattern by shifting belief away from the stronger alternative. The question extends beyond the scale \(\langle\textit{some},\textit{all}\rangle\). Gradable adjectives also require the listener to resolve an expression along a scale, and the endpoint-oriented interpretation of absolute adjectives raises the question of what produces this orientation (Xiang et al., 2022; see §1.7.3). These are phenomena to which a model of scalar resolution should aim to generalize.

Existing explanations of scalar implicature assign different roles to lexical meaning, alternatives, assumptions about speakers and world knowledge. Gricean reasoning relates the inference to the speaker's choice of a weaker expression when a stronger one was available (Grice, 1975; see §1.1), while grammatical accounts derive it through exhaustification (Chierchia et al., 2012; see §1.4). The Rational Speech Act framework (RSA) formalizes pragmatic interpretation as the recovery of a posterior over states by inversion of a speaker model (Goodman & Frank, 2016; see §1.2). These explanations make the relation between meaning and interpretation explicit, but assigning content to semantics or pragmatics does not by itself determine the process by which an interpretation is reached (§1.5.2). RSA, for example, specifies the posterior to be recovered without determining the resources, representations or information available to individual operations. How such a process operates is therefore a question distinct from what interpretation it produces.

To address this process, an account must specify what information each operation can use and how the representations involved are related. One possible restriction is locality, understood here as limiting the reach of a computation. What counts as local, and which aspect of a process is restricted, differs across accounts; §2.3 develops the relations and differences among these notions. Applied to scalar resolution, the general idea raises the first question of this paper: what computational constraint might the process obey? The order of computation presents a separate question. Is scalar resolution a single feedforward pass, understood here as the successive computation of lexical, situational and utility representations, or can the interpretation be reached through recurrent interaction? Considering these choices together raises a further question: can patterns of scalar resolution arise as algorithmic-level peculiarities? The term concerns how a model's representations and operations produce a pattern under constraints on computational resources. The term also covers questions about representation, processing time and halting that an algorithm must settle (§6.4).

These questions use Marr's (1982) distinction between a computational-level account, which specifies a goal, and an algorithmic-level account, which specifies representations and a process operating under resource constraints. The distinction is useful for posing the questions, but its boundaries require qualification. A computational goal cannot simply be assumed to be well defined independently of the algorithm that achieves it, nor an algorithmic specification independently of its implementation. The position taken here is that the three levels are at best a conjectural response to present limits of observability: no principled way is known of drawing, on a given model, the line at which the computational goal ends and the algorithmic specification begins. The paper nevertheless adopts the distinction in the absence of a better alternative. In the literature on predictive coding, it is also necessary to distinguish Bayesian inference from predictive coding, neither of which entails the other (Aitchison & Lengyel, 2017; see §2.1), and theory from circuit implementation (Spratling, 2013; see §2.4). A proposal about a process must therefore make clear which of these questions it addresses.

The thesis of this paper is that scalar resolution can be represented by a predictive-coding network whose structure is generated by local computation and the free-energy objective, and that such an architecture can account for scalar implicature through joint settlement while predicting the extreme-favouring posterior of complete-scale adjectives from its scale structure.[^terms] The present proposal concerns the free-energy objective and the predictive-coding algorithm and does not aim to extend its particular specifications to the implementation level of neural circuits. This extension would require further empirical work and a more detailed specification of how the proposed computations are realized in neural circuits. In the proposed architecture, the utterance clamps the lexical representation. At a fixed value of the learned parameter, the situation and utility states then settle jointly as the unique maximizer of one objective (see §3.4; notebook text cell 3 of `main.ipynb`, hereafter nbtc 3, §§4, 8.1). Scalar resolution in this architecture is consequently not a single feedforward pass that first settles a literal interpretation and then revises it pragmatically. Scalar implicature can arise from the joint settlement without explicit within-trial competition among alternative utterances (§4.1; nbtc 4, Part C). The extreme-favouring posterior of complete-scale adjectives, in turn, is predicted independently of settlement (§4.2.3). The maximum and minimum entries have projections on the utility basis that differ only in the sign of the even, width-sensitive coordinate (§§3.5 and 4.2.5; `main.ipynb` appendix F, hereafter nb appdx. F, §7).

The account attributes the production of scalar implicature to recurrent settling under the constraint of local computation. The architectural choices are motivated by the avoidance of unbounded inference: joint settlement avoids an unbounded number of levels of recursive inference (§3.1), and the closed scale provides a bounded domain for scalar values (§3.2). Describing the patterns as algorithmic-level peculiarities locates their explanation in these choices of representation and process. Whether the proposed architecture represents the brain's internal architecture, however, remains an empirical question. Different representations and processes may predict the same scalar implicature, including processes with different commitments to recurrence or locality. The relation between architectures and predicted behaviour is therefore many-to-one: reproducing the pattern alone does not identify the architecture that produces it in the brain (§2.5). The model provides a candidate explanation whose fidelity to human processing must be assessed through evidence that discriminates among the competing representational and processing commitments.

Several features of the present architecture delimit this explanatory claim. The implicature result depends on the number of atoms and on stated boundary conditions (§§4.1.2 and 4.1.7). The lexical representation is fixed rather than jointly inferred, so how the process relates to the semantic–pragmatic boundary lies outside the scope of the present architecture (§3.3; nbtc 3 §4). Although the architecture satisfies local computation, local plasticity requires a separate qualification. This locality constraint on parameter learning would be satisfied in a one-dimensional utility implementation; at the two utility dimensions used here, however, the update of the single plastic parameter is non-local (§3.4). This unresolved departure, together with the asymmetry between minimum and maximum adjective classes that the architecture does not predict (§§4.2.4 and 4.2.6), motivates an additional level representing alternatives (§5.2). The proposal and its accompanying conjectures suggest how strengthening might be related to resource constraints, through an explicit alternative whose effect on mass shift remains to be derived (§§5.2–5.3). Conjectures I to IV are the four proposals designated as conjectures because they are not yet implemented and checked quantitatively. They serve as the completion of the proposed model, outside the thesis concerning the present architecture. In Lakatos's (1970) terms, they form its positive heuristic: suggestions for how the architecture is to be developed, each stated with its premises and what remains to be derived (§5.4).

The paper develops the argument in this order. §1 examines the phenomena of scalar resolution, the Bayesian pragmatic and grammatical explanations, the limits of their division, and open questions about alternatives and scales. §2 introduces predictive coding under the free-energy principle, the challenges facing the framework, and the paper's position on elicitation and stipulations. This background leads to the specification of the architecture and its design strategy in §3, including how its function differs from that specified by RSA (§3.7), followed by the evaluation of scalar implicature and adjective scale classes in §4. The proposal of the additional level and the four conjectures follows in §5. §6 then considers the interpretation of the scale-class comparison (§6.1), realizability and halting (§6.2), cost in the dynamics (§6.3), what the algorithm specifies (§6.4), limitations (§6.5) and predictions (§6.6), before concluding in §6.7.

[^terms]: The terms used for the proposal differ in generality. *The simulation* is the Python implementation in `main.ipynb`. *The architecture* is the mathematical model as presently specified, comprising the simulation and the convergence and identifiability claims that do not depend on it. *The model* comprises all proposed mechanisms, whether specified or underspecified. *The network* is the architecture regarded as units and connections. *The construction* names the general constraints that the free-energy principle imposes on a predictive-coding network, set out in §2.2, following nbtc 3, preamble item 4. The simulation instantiates the architecture, the architecture instantiates the model, and the model instantiates the construction. *The process* is the true process of scalar resolution that the proposal concerns; it is not part of the proposal. # 1. Scalar resolution and the limits of the existing division

## 1.0 The explananda

The object of explanation is scalar resolution, the selection of a value on a scale (§0). The literature establishes or disputes four patterns to which an account of this process should generalize. With *some*, the interpretation falls outside the region of *all* (§1.1), and its dependence on the listener's prior is weaker than a Bayesian baseline predicts (§1.3.1). With absolute gradable adjectives, thresholds lie at or near a scale endpoint and interpretations tend towards this endpoint (§1.7.3). Whether antonymous absolute adjectives divide the scale without remainder is disputed: some degrees may satisfy neither antonym (§1.7.3). A further question concerns the lexicalization of the scale. The *not all* corner of the square of opposition, conventionally labelled O, lacks a single-word expression (§1.7.2). The first two patterns are evaluated in §§4.1 and 4.2. The third arises in §4.2's data and, together with the fourth, motivates the conjectures in §5.

These explananda are graded. The evaluations ask where a belief settles on the scale and whether it has moved, rather than treating an interpretation solely as the presence or absence of a reading (§§4.1.2 and 4.2.0). To establish what existing explanations contribute to these questions, §1 first develops the scalar-implicature problem and the probabilistic account of it, including the liabilities of prior-based strengthening (§§1.1–1.3). It then examines grammatical exhaustification (§1.4), the classification that divides the probabilistic and grammatical accounts (§1.5), and the alternatives that both employ (§1.6). The discussion of scales (§1.7) and of the status of a statement that is not false (§1.8) supplies the remaining background for the account of scalar resolution.

## 1.1 Scalar implicature

The inference from *some \(P\)* to *not all \(P\)* is systematic but defeasible (Levinson, 2000, pp. 16, 42). A speaker can cancel the inference explicitly with a rider, or a context can prevent the inference from arising (Geurts, 2010, p. 19, following Grice). The weak, unenriched meaning, or *prejacent*, therefore has to remain available. For a predicate \(P\) over the relevant domain, the contrast is between \(\exists x\,P(x)\) and the strengthened interpretation \(\exists x\,P(x)\wedge\neg\forall x\,P(x)\). Encoding the latter as the invariant truth-conditional meaning of *some* would lose the weak interpretation that cancellation requires. The explanatory question is how the stronger interpretation becomes available while the weak meaning is preserved.

The Gricean account approaches this question through the speaker's choice. If an informed speaker could have truthfully used *all* but chose *some*, the listener may reason from the maxim of Quantity that *all* was not assertable (Grice, 1975, pp. 45–46; Katzir, 2007, p. 672). The crucial distinction is between two epistemic conclusions. The weak, or primary, inference is that the speaker does not believe the alternative with *all*, which leaves open the possibility that the speaker is ignorant. The strong, or secondary, inference is that the speaker believes this alternative to be false. Reaching the strong inference requires a further epistemic step that the maxims themselves do not supply (Sauerland, 2004, pp. 383, 388; Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2300). Grammatical accounts locate the source of the strong inference in exhaustification, an operation that strengthens a meaning by excluding alternatives, rather than in reasoning about the speaker's beliefs (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2297, 2300; see §1.4).

The need for this additional step becomes clearer when scalar implicatures are compared with ignorance implicatures. Gricean reasoning yields inferences that the speaker is unsure of an alternative, as with each disjunct in *p or q* (Chierchia, 2017, pp. 246–248). To obtain the scalar inference, the speaker must be assumed to have an opinion about the relevant alternative. Yet this assumption must apply selectively: applied to the disjuncts, the assumption yields a contradiction (Chierchia, 2017, p. 249). The two kinds of inference also differ in their behaviour. A scalar implicature remains available when the maxim of Quantity is plainly suspended, as in an utterance by a quiz host who knows the answer, whereas the corresponding ignorance inference is unavailable (Fox, 2014, as cited in Chierchia, 2017, p. 250). Scalar implicatures are also easier to cancel than ignorance implicatures (Chierchia, 2017, p. 253). On the grammatical view, ignorance inferences can be left to Gricean reasoning or obtained by exhaustifying over the speaker's beliefs; a scalar implicature and an ignorance implicature can then coexist (Chierchia, 2017, p. 257).

The form in which *some* occurs further affects the availability and strength of the inference. Contrastive stress on *some* forces the implicature (Degen & Tanenhaus, 2015, §1.5, n. 3), while the partitive *some of the* receives higher implicature-strength ratings than bare *some* without categorically requiring a proper-part reading (Degen, 2015, pp. 21–22). Proper partitivity is especially apparent with numerals: *two of my parents* is deviant when the numeral equals the size of the whole set (Barker, 1998, p. 703). Accounts disagree, however, about the source of this proper-part interpretation. Barker (1998, pp. 703–704) assigns the proper-part relation to partitive *of*, so that the whole's being larger than the named part is entailed. Marty (2019, pp. 143, 150–152, 154) instead assigns *of* a part-of relation that allows a whole to count as part of itself; the proper-part interpretation arises through competition with a definite alternative and behaves as a presupposition. Embedding also matters: under negation, a scalar item's enriched reading appears to require focal stress or a strong contextual bias (Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2305). These differences in form and context matter when interpreting evidence for an implicature. The distinction between testing for the implicature and testing for strengthening as a mechanism that produces it is developed in §4.1.2 (nbtc 4, Parts C and D).

A lexical account offers another way to preserve the weak meaning: *some* could be ambiguous, with *some but not all* as one of its readings. The explanatory objection, raised by Gazdar, is that multiplying lexical ambiguities misses a generalization (as cited in Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2309). Where a pragmatic account covers the same facts, Grice's Modified Occam's Razor favours deriving the interpretation from general principles over stipulating it in the lexicon (Geurts, 2010, p. 16). Whichever source of enrichment is adopted, an account must retain the weak meaning required by cancellation and explain where enrichment applies, including inside embedded expressions (§1.4). These requirements provide the basis for comparing probabilistic and grammatical accounts.

## 1.2 RSA as a computational-level theory

RSA formalizes pragmatic interpretation as Bayesian inversion of a speaker model (Goodman & Frank, 2016, pp. 819–820). In the RSA notation used here, \(s\) is a world state, \(u\) an utterance, \(P(s)\) the world state prior, and \(\llbracket u\rrbracket(s)\) an indicator of whether \(u\) is true at \(s\). The literal listener \(L_0\) conditions this prior on the utterance's truth:

\[
L_0(s\mid u)\propto\llbracket u\rrbracket(s)P(s). \tag{1.1}
\]

A pragmatic speaker \(S_1\) chooses an utterance by weighing how well it would lead this listener to the intended state against its cost. Let \(C(u)\) be the utterance cost and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{rsa}}\) the speaker's optimality parameter, which controls sensitivity to differences in utility; \(\alpha\) alone denotes the concentration parameter of the \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,1)\) base world prior used in the present architecture's evaluation (§4.1.1). With the cost refinement of Goodman and Frank (2016, Box 1, p. 822), the speaker model is

\[
S_1(u\mid s)\propto\exp\!\left\{\alpha_{\mathrm{rsa}}\left[\log L_0(s\mid u)-C(u)\right]\right\}. \tag{1.2}
\]

The pragmatic listener \(L_1\) then treats this speaker model as the likelihood of the observed utterance and updates the world state prior:

\[
L_1(s\mid u)\propto S_1(u\mid s)P(s). \tag{1.3}
\]

The speaker distribution is normalized over utterances, so the probability assigned to the observed utterance depends on the alternatives the speaker could have chosen. This dependence carries through to the pragmatic listener. In the familiar *some–all* case, *all* gives a cooperative speaker a more informative expression at the all-state; choosing *some* can consequently lower the listener's probability of this state (Goodman & Frank, 2016, pp. 820–821, 823–824). The effect depends on the speaker model and the world state prior, whose role is examined in §1.3. For the present proposal, the dependence on unobserved utterances also raises an architectural question. The objective specified in §3.7 contains no term referring to an unobserved utterance and thereby removes this within-trial dependency (nb appdx. B, *Alternative spaces*).

The sequence from \(L_0\) through \(S_1\) to \(L_1\) is the minimal recursion of the standard model. RSA's inferential goal does not, however, fix this depth: social recursion may be finite or continue indefinitely. The literal listener supplies a stopping point, and deeper levels of speaker and listener reasoning remain possible (Goodman & Frank, 2016, pp. 819–820, 823). RSA also supplies a substantive computational baseline. RSA has captured pragmatic effects in reference games (Frank & Goodman, 2012) and in implicature tasks that vary the speaker's knowledge (Goodman & Stuhlmüller, 2013, as cited in Goodman & Frank, 2016, pp. 823–824). The questions about RSA's dependence on priors, taken up in §1.3, arise against these successes.

## 1.3 Prior-based strengthening and its liabilities

### 1.3.1 Prior insensitivity

In the *some–all* comparison, weak *some* eliminates only the zero-state, while competition with *all* targets the all-state. A sufficiently concentrated world state prior can therefore continue to dominate the pragmatic listener's interpretation. Degen et al. (2015) tested this dependence using independently elicited priors and found less sensitivity to them than standard RSA predicted. Even items for which the elicited prior strongly favoured the all-state received low posterior probability for this state after *some*. Filler conditions indicated that participants were not simply ignoring world knowledge. Because the priors were elicited independently, their use in the baseline constituted an empirical prediction; flattening them after seeing the comprehension results would describe the discrepancy without explaining when or why flattening occurs. The discrepancy, however, concerns Degen et al.'s (2015) task and the tested baseline together with the assumption that the elicited prior is the one used during interpretation (cf. §2.5). Their response, wonky-world RSA, makes the applicability of this prior something the listener can infer (§1.3.2). The present architecture's dependence on its own base world prior is examined separately in §4.1.5: maintaining a not-all majority requires a minimum lexical strength, and this minimum increases with the concentration of the base world prior (nbtc 6).

### 1.3.2 Wonky-world RSA and the inverse comparison

Wonky-world RSA introduces a latent variable \(w\) that distinguishes an ordinary setting from a wonky one (Degen et al., 2015). In the ordinary setting, the conditional world state prior \(P(s\mid w)\) is the elicited prior; in the wonky setting, it is a uniform fallback. The listener infers the world state and the setting jointly:

\[
L_1(s,w\mid u)\propto S_1(u\mid s,w)P(s\mid w)P(w), \tag{1.4}
\]

where \(P(w)\) is the prior over the two settings and \(S_1(u\mid s,w)\) is the speaker model conditional on the state and setting. An unexpected utterance can thus lead the listener to reconsider whether the ordinary world state prior applies.

To construct the ordinary prior, Degen et al. (2015) collected one response per participant after a one-sentence scenario, for example that fifteen marbles had been thrown into a pool. Participants used a slider from 0 to 15 to judge how many objects showed the relevant effect. Each item's 12 to 29 judgements were pooled and smoothed to form a distribution over the sixteen possible counts. This distribution supplied the ordinary prior, while speaker optimality, denoted \(\lambda\) in that study, and the prior probability of wonkiness were fitted to the comprehension data by mean squared error. The authors' public repository also contains a second elicitation for the same 90 items, unreported in the 2015 paper. Participants supplied a lowest count, a highest count, a best guess and a confidence rating. The prior constructed from the best guesses alone was again pooled from one response per participant and smoothed in the same way (Degen, 2019). §4.1.1 computes the comparison between these two elicited priors.

The wording of the comprehension task introduces a further qualification. Participants heard a partitive utterance, such as *Some of the marbles sank*, and judged how many of the fifteen objects showed the effect (Degen, 2019, experiment code). The prior-insensitivity finding in §1.3.1 is consequently a finding about *some of the*. The differences between bare *some* and the partitive reviewed in §1.1 must be kept in view when using this evidence (cf. §6.6). §3.7 develops the relation of the present architecture to wonky-world RSA, to RSA's literal listener, and to the quantity that RSA's speaker model reads.

### 1.3.3 Anti-exhaustivity

A different liability concerns the direction in which interpretation changes a belief. Under a sufficiently skewed world state prior, baseline RSA can predict *anti-exhaustivity*: the pragmatic listener becomes more confident in a state that the exhaustive interpretation excludes than the listener was before the utterance (Cremers et al., 2023). Anti-exhaustivity is distinct from the mismatch in Degen et al. (2015), where the baseline left the all-state too probable without increasing its probability (Cremers et al., 2023). Cremers et al. (2023) tested exhaustivity using a conjunctive alternative, *the sandwich* against *the sandwich and the soup*, rather than *some* against *all*. They found no anti-exhaustivity effect, although message choice was sensitive to priors. Models with an encapsulated exhaustivity mechanism provided the best fits. The preference for encapsulated exhaustivity among the tested models provides an independent reason to question whether strengthening should be computed directly from the listener's current prior. However, this model comparison does not uniquely identify a covert grammatical operator. A similar direction of change also appears empirically in the present architecture's normalized read-out: for some base world priors, the all-region gains probability relative to the literal listener (§4.1.4; nbtc 4b). Tempering produces this direction of change in the architecture, whereas the world state prior acts through the speaker model in RSA. The parallel concerns direction only, since the reference distributions also differ (§4.1.4).

## 1.4 Grammatical exhaustification and embedded readings

### 1.4.1 What “grammatical” names: the operator and its insertion sites

Grammatical accounts locate the derivation of scalar implicatures within the composition of sentence meaning. Chierchia et al. (2012, pp. 2297–2298) align the semantics–pragmatics divide with the distinction between compositional and postcompositional processes: compositional operations apply automatically, without consulting speaker intentions or contextual knowledge. Scalar alternatives are represented compositionally, and a covert exhaustivity operator, akin to *only*, can occur at different positions in logical form, the syntactic representation interpreted by the semantics. The operator makes enrichment part of the meaning composed at its insertion site, so the strong scalar inference of §1.1 can be derived, including inside a larger expression.

Chierchia et al. (2012) write the operator as O; this paper uses \(\mathsf{Exh}\). Their initial definition takes a sentence \(S\) and a set of alternatives \(\mathrm{ALT}\), and conjoins \(S\) with the negation of every alternative that \(S\) does not entail (their definition (15), p. 2304). Applied to *some* with *all* as the stronger alternative, exhaustification yields the strengthened truth conditions by excluding *all*. This definition requires refinement, however, when alternatives are symmetric. If both disjuncts are alternatives to *p or q*, neither is entailed by the disjunction, but denying both contradicts it. The revised operator therefore negates only the *innocently excludable* alternatives: those belonging to every maximal set of alternatives that can jointly be denied consistently with \(S\) (Chierchia et al., 2012, definition (53), pp. 2314–2315). Whenever the initial definition yields a consistent result, the two definitions coincide. Innocent exclusion preserves the operation of strengthening while preventing the indiscriminate exclusion of alternatives that cannot all be false together.

The claim that composition is blind to context does not imply complete independence from contextual information. Variables in a logical form, including the domain of a quantifier, receive their values from context through a postcompositional process (Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2298). Alternatives are generated by grammatical principles and, in typical cases, constrained lexically by Horn scales (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2299, 2301). The computation of scalar implicatures then uses logical entailment to determine informativity, rather than entailment relative to contextual knowledge. In this sense, the computation is encapsulated from this knowledge (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2319–2321; Fox & Hackl, 2006, and Magri, 2009, as cited in Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2321).[^contextblind] Distinguishing the operation from the contextual determination of its inputs will be essential to locating where world knowledge enters the account.

Because \(\mathsf{Exh}\) is an operator at logical form, its insertion at the root or at an embedded constituent can produce different truth conditions. Candidate sites must therefore be compared. Interpretations that add information are generally preferred, although coherence can license weakening (Chierchia, 2017, p. 259, parsing condition (34)). A conditional illustrates why the site matters. Exhaustification in the consequent can strengthen the conditional, whereas exhaustification in its downward-entailing antecedent can weaken it. Even in the latter case, context can force a locally exhaustified interpretation if the unexhaustified interpretation would be contradictory (Chierchia, 2017, pp. 258–259; Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2306–2307). Here *local* identifies an embedded site of exhaustification.[^local]

### 1.4.2 Where world knowledge enters

If exhaustification is blind to contextual knowledge, what determines whether an interpretation adds information or is coherent? Chierchia et al. (2012) supply different answers for these two judgements. Information is assessed by logical strength, which requires a set of worlds but no measure over them. Their candidate principle for choosing among parses, the Strongest Meaning Hypothesis, compares a sentence containing an occurrence of the exhaustivity operator with the sentence obtained by removing this occurrence. Other things being equal, the version without the operator is preferred if it is logically stronger (Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2327; Dalrymple et al., 1998, as cited in Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2326). This principle does not, however, rank every pair of insertion sites. In upward-entailing contexts, both matrix and embedded exhaustification may strengthen the unexhaustified sentence without belonging to each other's comparison class. The principle then leaves their relative preference open, and the chapter allows more general considerations, including a reading's plausibility, to decide (Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2327).

Coherence concerns non-contradiction. An operator that applies without consulting contextual knowledge can produce an interpretation that contradicts what the context establishes; the chapter treats the resulting oddness as evidence for the grammatical account (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2319–2321). Detecting this contradiction requires a subsequent comparison with the context. The operator's blindness thus has a precise scope: it computes over logical entailment without consulting contextual knowledge or a probability measure. Interpretation as a whole, however, still draws on context to assign values to variables, to detect contradictions, and to judge plausibility where logical strength leaves a choice open. World knowledge enters at these points of contextual resolution, selection and assessment, even though it does not enter the exhaustification operation itself. The next question is what representation of world knowledge these judgements require.

### 1.4.3 The context set and the Bayesian prior

Stalnaker (1978/1999, p. 84) represents the common ground by a *context set*, the set of worlds compatible with what is taken for granted in the conversation. This set is not a priori: determining whether a proposition \(x\) is presupposed, and hence true at every world in the context set, requires resolving the content of \(x\) against prior knowledge. Dependence on prior knowledge does not, however, make a context set a Bayesian prior. Let \(W\) be the space of worlds. A context set is a subset \(C\subseteq W\), whereas a Bayesian prior is a probability measure \(P\) on a \(\sigma\)-algebra of propositions over \(W\). The two are different kinds of object.

Their relationship can be stated precisely on a finite \(W\), the setting adopted for the following comparison. A prior determines its support, the set of worlds to which it assigns positive probability, but this set does not determine how probability is distributed among its members. The map from priors to supports is therefore many-to-one. If \(C\) is the support of \(P\), updating the context set by intersecting it with an utterance's truth set gives the support of the conditioned prior. Writing \(\llbracket u\rrbracket\) for the truth set of utterance \(u\),

\[
\operatorname{supp}P(\cdot\mid\llbracket u\rrbracket)
=\operatorname{supp}P\cap\llbracket u\rrbracket
=C\cap\llbracket u\rrbracket,
\qquad P(\llbracket u\rrbracket)>0.
\tag{1.5}
\]

The positive-probability condition matters: if the utterance's truth set has probability zero, Bayesian conditioning is undefined, whereas the corresponding intersection empties the context set (nb appdx. D §6). Equation (1.5) identifies what the two updates share without equating a set with a measure. The difference between the two representations emerges in the grading of admitted worlds. A context set alone ranks none of its worlds above another, so it cannot supply the plausibility judgement invoked when logical strength does not determine a preferred reading (§1.4.2). Probabilities are one way to supply a grading, but an ordering without probabilities could also do so, as with Kratzer's (1981, pp. 47–48) ordering source or Spohn's (1988, p. 115) ordinal conditional functions. In either case, ranking readings additionally requires a rule relating the grading of worlds to the readings these worlds support. A plausibility judgement therefore requires more than a context set, though not necessarily a probability measure. Existing frameworks connect sets and measures in different ways. RSA's literal listener in Eq. (1.1) restricts its prior to an utterance's truth set and normalizes the remaining probability (Goodman & Frank, 2016). Probabilistic treatments of context can add structure to the representation of common ground itself: Yalcin (2012, pp. 131, 135–136) represents a conversational state by sets of probability spaces, where the elementary picture has a set of propositions.

The shared exclusion structure can also be described as a *kernel*. For a homomorphism, the kernel consists of the elements of its domain sent to zero. Here propositions are subsets \(A\) of \(W\), and restriction to the context set acts as the Boolean homomorphism \(h_C(A)=A\cap C\). Its zero is the empty set, so its kernel contains exactly the propositions incompatible with \(C\). The propositions of probability zero similarly form the kernel of the quotient homomorphism associated with \(P\); the measure itself is not a Boolean homomorphism (Givant & Halmos, 2009, pp. 153, 178, 291–292; nb appdx. D §6). On a finite world space, when \(C=\operatorname{supp}P\),

\[
\ker h_C
=\{A\subseteq W:A\cap C=\varnothing\}
=\{A\subseteq W:P(A)=0\}.
\tag{1.6}
\]

The context set and the prior then exclude the same propositions. They differ in the grading of the worlds that remain admitted, which the measure supplies and the set does not. This finite-space comparison is the sense in which the two objects share a kernel; nb appdx. D §6 gives the construction and explains why null sets require a different treatment on continuous spaces.

### 1.4.4 The empirical record on embedded readings

Embedded readings provide evidence about where enrichment can occur. Hurford-type disjunctions provide one argument: where the weak meanings would make one disjunct entail the other, embedded enrichment can remove this entailment and explain the disjunction's acceptability (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2309–2310). Contextually supported conditionals provide another. Across tasks and constructions, however, studies have reached different conclusions about the availability and interpretation of local readings (Chemla & Spector, 2011; Clifton & Dube, 2010; Geurts & Pouscoulous, 2009; Ippolito, 2010; Magri, 2011). These findings have not established a unique assignment of the phenomenon to semantics or pragmatics. Lexical-uncertainty versions of RSA can derive embedded implicatures pragmatically by representing uncertainty about lexical meanings (Bergen et al., 2016; Potts et al., 2016; see §1.5.1). The existence of an embedded reading therefore identifies something an account must explain, while leaving a choice among mechanisms capable of producing it.

[^local]: Grammatical *locality* concerns exhaustification at an embedded constituent rather than at the sentence root (Chierchia et al., 2012, p. 2310). Bogacz's (2017) constraints concern different objects: local computation limits a unit's update to quantities available at that unit, and local plasticity limits a synaptic update to the activities the synapse connects (§2.3.1). Locality in Chomskian syntax instead restricts how far a syntactic dependency can reach (e.g., Rizzi, 2001, p. 89). There may be a similar conceptual motivation behind the syntactic and computational restrictions; this paper notes the resemblance and reserves its position on it (§2.3).

[^contextblind]: The terminating prior of the utility state in the present architecture, \(\mu_u\), also satisfies this criterion of context-blindness: its value is fixed and is not set by speaker intentions or contextual knowledge (§3.3; nbtc 3 §3, item 5). This property leaves open whether the process represented by the architecture is compositional or postcompositional. Resolving the output of exhaustification against context also has an analogue in the lexical–situation feedback path, where the fixed lexical entry meets the base world prior in a residual (§3.2; nbtc 3 §4; nb appdx. D §5). These parallels raise the question of whether the stated properties suffice to identify grammar; they do not supply a classification of the present architecture.

## 1.5 The demarcation problem

### 1.5.1 The classification, and the tension between the two accounts

Debates about scalar implicature commonly ask whether the pattern belongs to semantics or pragmatics and what evidence could determine this assignment. Three criteria often enter the classification: truth-conditionality, conventionality and contextual constancy. Yet these criteria cross-cut one another. Literal content and conversational implicature receive relatively stable classifications, while indexicals, pragmatic enrichment and expressive content do not (Gutzmann, 2021; Jaszczolt, 2012). Scalar implicature occupies the contested region. Cancellability and conversational derivation support a pragmatic classification, whereas truth-conditional effects under embedding have been used to support a semantic one. Pragmatic, lexical and grammatical analyses consequently remain live options (Sauerland, 2012).

The comparison between standard RSA and grammatical accounts gives the dispute a more concrete form. Standard RSA places recursive reasoning over whole-utterance alternatives after composition (Goodman & Frank, 2016). Grammatical exhaustification instead operates within composition at positions in logical form (Chierchia et al., 2012). Embedded implicature is therefore available to the grammatical account wherever the operator can be inserted, subject to the selection of readings described in §1.4. Standard RSA requires additional machinery to derive embedded implicatures, such as lexical uncertainty (Bergen et al., 2016; Potts et al., 2016). The source of alternatives also differs between the versions compared here: standard RSA takes a set stipulated by the modeller, whereas structural accounts generate candidates through grammatical operations (Katzir, 2007; see §1.6).

The accounts also represent world knowledge and interpretive outcomes differently. RSA's world state prior enters both the literal and pragmatic listeners, Eqs. (1.1) and (1.3), and its support can play the role of a context set. The grammatical operator consults neither this measure nor contextual knowledge. Assessing coherence requires the context set, while choosing a plausible reading where logical strength does not decide requires additional grading (§§1.4.2–1.4.3). RSA returns a graded posterior over worlds; grammatical exhaustification supplies a truth set for each insertion site, which updates the context set by intersection. This distinction in output has a precise consequence for the kernel. Under the conditions specified next, RSA preserves the literal listener's zero-probability propositions while reweighting the admitted worlds. Exhaustification of *some* changes the truth set by excluding the all-worlds and thus enlarges the kernel whenever such worlds were previously admitted.

### 1.5.2 The tension read through the kernel, and what the classification leaves open

The kernel comparison follows from the RSA equations under explicit conditions. Consider the finite-world case with \(0<\alpha_{\mathrm{rsa}}<\infty\), finite utterance costs and \(P(\llbracket u\rrbracket)>0\). Assume that the literal listener is defined for the candidate utterances and that the speaker distribution has a finite, positive normalizer at each state in the prior's support. Wherever \(L_0(s\mid u)>0\), its logarithm is finite, so the speaker weight in Eq. (1.2) is strictly positive. Equation (1.3) then gives positive pragmatic-listener probability at these states. At an admitted state where \(L_0(s\mid u)=0\), the corresponding speaker weight is zero, using the limiting value of the exponential in Eq. (1.2), so the pragmatic listener also assigns zero probability. States outside the prior's support have zero probability for both listeners. Thus

\[
L_1(s\mid u)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_0(s\mid u)>0.
\tag{1.7}
\]

On the finite world space, Eq. (1.7) means that the two listeners have the same kernel of null propositions. RSA's scalar implicature is a regrading of the admitted worlds, with no additional exclusion. In the *some–all* comparison, with the prior and costs held fixed, exclusion can be approached only as \(\alpha_{\mathrm{rsa}}\) tends to infinity, and then only where *all* is an optimal utterance at the all-state given these costs. By contrast, exhaustification changes the truth set itself. With the *some–all* alternatives,

\[
\llbracket\mathsf{Exh}(\textit{some})\rrbracket
=\llbracket\textit{some}\rrbracket\setminus\llbracket\textit{all}\rrbracket.
\tag{1.8}
\]

Updating with this strengthened meaning excludes all-worlds that remain compatible with the weak meaning. Equations (1.7) and (1.8) thus distinguish regrading from a change in what the interpretation admits. Reading the comparison through §1.4.3 narrows the disagreement about world knowledge. When the context set is the support of RSA's prior, the two representations already agree on which worlds are excluded before the utterance. RSA additionally grades the admitted worlds, whereas the context set by itself supplies no grading. The accounts then implement scalar implicature at different levels of this representation: grammatical exhaustification changes the kernel, while RSA changes the weights and preserves the kernel. At the point where the grammatical account appeals to plausibility, however, it needs a grading beyond its context set. A probability measure could supply one, but Chierchia et al. (2012, p. 2327) do not specify which kind of grading their plausibility judgement uses. The contrast is asymmetric at this point: the grammatical account appeals to a resource that RSA already represents. The two approaches can nevertheless be combined. Franke and Bergen (2020) use grammatically generated readings as the hypothesis space for probabilistic choice, and Champollion et al. (2019) apply RSA to exhaustified meanings. Both combine readings represented as sets with a measure over readings. The comparison therefore does not force an exclusive choice between grammatical and probabilistic explanation.

For the present paper, the further question is what the semantic–pragmatic classification leaves open. Classifying a dependency between kinds of content does not determine how the dependency is computed: although RSA's \(L_1\) depends on \(L_0\) through \(S_1\), this dependence does not by itself establish successive processing stages. The present architecture offers a recurrent implementation of downstream interpretation without imposing such stages (§3.4; nbtc 3 §8). Its recurrence does not decide the classification, and its lexical representation is fixed rather than jointly inferred. How the process relates to the semantic–pragmatic boundary consequently remains outside the scope of the present architecture (§3.3; nbtc 3 §4).

## 1.6 The source and bounds of alternatives

Both probabilistic and grammatical accounts assign an explanatory role to alternatives, which raises questions about their number, membership, selection and computational cost. The first concerns the bounds on a candidate set. In the lexical-scale accounts of Horn (1972) and Gazdar (1979, both as cited in Katzir, 2007, pp. 674–675), the minimal configuration contains the prejacent and at least one stronger alternative; without the latter there is nothing to exclude. The proposals reviewed here specify no numerical maximum. Katzir (2007, pp. 678–679) instead restricts candidates structurally: an alternative is obtained through a finite sequence of deletions, contractions and replacements by constituents of the same category drawn from a substitution source. In the initial version, this source is the lexicon, so no alternative is structurally more complex than the prejacent and a finite lexicon yields a finite set. The final version adds subtrees of the prejacent to the substitution source, allowing an alternative to be strictly larger than the prejacent (Katzir, 2007, p. 685). The earlier size bound therefore cannot simply be carried over to the revised definition.

Restrictions on candidate generation are needed to address the symmetry problem (Fox & Katzir, 2011; Breheny et al., 2018). If every stronger sentence were an alternative to *some*, the candidates would include both *all* and *some but not all*. Given *some*, these two alternatives cannot both be false. In the speaker-reasoning derivation, treating them symmetrically would yield ignorance about both rather than the attested *not all* inference, so the latter derivation requires keeping *some but not all* out of the alternative set (Katzir, 2007, pp. 672–674). Restricting the set does not imply reducing every case to two members: evidence that numerals compete with *some* shows competition beyond a two-member scale (Degen & Tanenhaus, 2015). In RSA, the modeller fixes which candidates enter the set (Goodman & Frank, 2016).

The question of membership is distinct from the question of size. Lexical-scale accounts make each member of the relevant scale an alternative (Horn, 1972, as cited in Katzir, 2007, pp. 674–675). Implicature rates differ across scales, the pattern known as *scalar diversity* (van Tiel et al., 2016). Such variation does not by itself establish different membership rules, since accessibility, relevance, cost or lexical semantics could produce different rates under the same rule. Van Tiel et al. (2016) relate the variation to properties of scales, including whether the stronger term is bounded and how far apart the terms are in meaning. Katzir's (2007) structural account uses a different basis for membership: any same-category constituent available from the substitution source can generate a candidate, so the set can extend beyond a Horn scale. When candidates are used, the conversational principle selects for strength and weak assertability (Katzir, 2007, p. 679, principle (21)). Generation and use therefore impose different restrictions.

How these restrictions select the relevant alternatives is the next issue. Alternative sets depend on context as well as on the uttered expression (Rooth, 1985, 1992; Kratzer & Shimoyama, 2002). Structural constraints generate candidates, while competition operates among the generated candidates; strength and assertability filter the set when it is used (Katzir, 2007; Fox & Spector, 2018). Relevance can restrict the candidates further (Fox & Katzir, 2011). These roles should also be distinguished from accessibility: Ronai and Xiang (2024) find that alternative accessibility behaves as an exposure-sensitive quantity, whereas contextual relevance governs inference rates. A theory of alternative selection must therefore explain which candidates are available and which of these matter in the context.

Finally, computing and selecting alternatives takes effort, raising the question of what motivates a system to undertake it. Proposed motivations include cooperation (Grice, 1975), speaker and hearer economy (Horn, 1984), defaults that spare repeated computation (Levinson, 2000), and a trade-off between effort and effect under which enrichment is derived when relevant (Sperber & Wilson, 1995). Levinson's (2000, pp. 16, 36, 42) generalized implicatures remain tied to alternatives: an utterance normally carries the inference over a salient contrast set, such as \(\langle\textit{all},\textit{some}\rangle\), unless unusual contextual assumptions defeat it. The default is thus defeasible and keyed to a set. Bott and Noveck (2004) measured a processing cost for scalar implicature. Whether a system can infer the cost of its own computation is a further question, taken up in §6.3.

## 1.7 Scales: definition, structure, thresholds, and extreme-favouring interpretation

### 1.7.1 What a scale is

The discussion of alternatives leaves two questions about scales: what kind of object constitutes a scale, and what makes a lexical item belong to a particular one? The literature assigns several kinds of structure to scales, but these proposals often answer different questions. A theory of how alternatives are generated need not compete with a theory of how degrees are ordered or of which operations a measurement permits. Katzir's (2007) account illustrates the first possibility. It takes no scale as a primitive: membership in an alternative set depends on structural derivability, and the set need not come with an ordering (§1.6). Horn (1972) and Gazdar (1979), as cited in Katzir (2007, pp. 674–675), by contrast, organize expressions into linearly ordered tuples under asymmetric entailment. Membership conditions include belonging to the same semantic field; Matsumoto (1995, as cited in Katzir, 2007, p. 676) additionally requires a shared direction of scalarity, with the expressions all positively or all negatively scalar (see also Levinson, 2000). Linearity, however, is not a requirement in every account. Hirschberg (1985, p. 125) allows scalar inferences over partially ordered sets of expressions or values. A linear order compares every pair, whereas a partial order can leave some pairs incomparable (Davey & Priestley, 2002). At the level of propositions, entailment supplies the order of a Boolean algebra. A linearly ordered scale is then a chain within this algebra; a partially ordered collection need not be a chain.

Degree semantics shifts the focus from expressions and propositions to ordered values. Kennedy and McNally (2005, pp. 351, 354) characterize a scale by a set of degrees, an ordering relation and a dimension, with the ordered degrees represented by a real interval (see also Kennedy, 2007). These components distinguish scales: *tall* and *short* share their degrees and dimension but order them in opposite directions. Whether the degree set has a minimum and a maximum determines four structural classes, represented by the totally open interval \((0,1)\), the lower-closed interval \([0,1)\), the upper-closed interval \((0,1]\), and the totally closed interval \([0,1]\). An adjective's lexical entry specifies its scale. Measurement theories ask a further question about the operations licensed by such a representation. Ordinal, interval and ratio scales support different operations (Sassoon, 2010; R. van Rooij, 2011). Representing interval or ratio measurements by real numbers does not require the degree set itself to form an ordered field.

These accounts make it necessary to distinguish a scale of expressions from a scale of values. Scalar implicature is usually formulated over expressions, such as *some* and *all*, while threshold semantics concerns degrees or proportions. Nor does a value scale have to be dense. For a quantifier ranging over a finite domain of \(n\) individuals, a count \(k\), from zero to \(n\), yields a proportion \(k/n\). The available proportions form a finite set, so a dense representation is an idealization. The choice of scale therefore includes both the kind of object being ordered and the granularity of its values.

### 1.7.2 The square of opposition and its unlexicalized corner

The square of opposition organizes a pair of expression scales, \(\langle\textit{all},\textit{some}\rangle\) and \(\langle\textit{none},\textit{not all}\rangle\). The strong members occupy the A and E corners, respectively, and the weak members occupy I and O (Levinson, 2000, p. 65). Levinson's *none* corresponds to *no* in this paper's inventory. Two statements are *contraries* when they cannot both be true but can both be false; they are *contradictories* when they can neither both be true nor both be false (Levinson, 2000, pp. 64–65). On a non-empty domain that permits an intermediate case, *all* and *none* are contraries. *All* and *not all* are contradictories, as are *none* and *some* (Levinson, 2000, pp. 65–66). Modal expressions and connectives can be organized by the same square (Levinson, 2000, pp. 66–67).

Although the square defines four logical corners, their lexicalization is asymmetric. Horn's generalization is that the O corner, *not all*, lacks a single-word expression, both among quantifiers and in other categories organized by the square (Horn, 1972, as cited in Katzir & Singh, 2013; Horn, 1989, as cited in Levinson, 2000, p. 69). English has no *nall*, *nand* or *noth* corresponding to *none*, *nor* and *neither*. Horn explains the missing expression by the availability of the same content as an implicature of *some*. The implicature explanation alone, however, leaves the choice between two inventories unresolved: an inventory lexicalizing *not all* could instead leave *some* to be conveyed by implicature. The proposed tie-break favours the inventory containing fewer negations (Katzir & Singh, 2013). Levinson (2000, p. 70) states the explanation in two steps: content conveyed by a generalized implicature need not be lexicalized, and when a positive and a negative term carry the same load, the positive term is taken as basic because the negative term is more complex.

### 1.7.3 Adjective classes, pragmatic model fits and unresolved questions

Xiang et al. (2022) found distinct patterns of scalar interpretation across adjective classes. When participants inferred an object's degree from an utterance containing a gradable adjective, maximum-standard adjectives elicited choices concentrated at the maximum, minimum-standard adjectives elicited choices across non-minimal degrees, and relative adjectives elicited choices mainly above the midpoint (Xiang et al., 2022, §2.3.2). These classes differ in their standards: maximum-standard adjectives conventionally require the maximal degree of a property, minimum-standard adjectives a non-zero degree, and relative adjectives a context-dependent threshold (Kennedy & McNally, 2005, p. 356; Kennedy, 2007).

The RSA models examined by Xiang et al. (2022) captured these posterior degree judgements relatively well, but their fits to truth-value judgements revealed a class asymmetry: the Lassiter and Goodman (2017) model, LG, was weaker for maximum-standard adjectives, whereas the Qing and Franke (2014) model, QF, was weaker for minimum-standard adjectives (Xiang et al., 2022, §4). The models differ in how they determine the threshold against which a degree is evaluated. In LG, the pragmatic listener jointly infers the degree and threshold, starting from a uniform prior over thresholds. In QF, as implemented by Xiang et al., the threshold distribution is a convention derived from expected communicative success, and the listener reasons about a speaker who already knows this distribution (Xiang et al., 2022, §1.2.2). Thus, the models' success in predicting communicated degrees does not extend uniformly to judgements of when an adjective applies.

To investigate the weaker truth-value fits, Xiang et al. (2022, §4) reconstructed threshold distributions from the mean judgements under a hypothesis linking truth conditions to responses. Maximum-standard thresholds concentrated at the maximum, while minimum-standard thresholds favoured the first non-zero position on the five-image scales. These endpoint preferences were stronger for geometric shapes than for familiar artefacts. The reconstructed distributions accord with semantic accounts that associate closed-scale adjectives with endpoint-oriented thresholds, in contrast to the context-dependent thresholds of open-scale relative adjectives (Kennedy, 2007). Xiang et al. consequently argue for combining Bayesian inference with semantic conventions governing thresholds. What produces endpoint orientation remains a question for a model of scalar resolution (§§4.2 and 6.1).

Endpoint orientation also leaves open how the truth regions of antonyms relate. A maximum-standard adjective and its minimum-standard antonym need not divide the scale without remainder merely because their standards are associated with endpoints. Rotstein and Winter (2004, pp. 259, 265, 280) treat total–partial pairs such as *dry* and *wet* as complementary only by default: the total adjective's standard is fixed at the lower bound of the partial adjective's scale, but the partial adjective's standard can lie elsewhere, allowing a moist towel to count as neither wet nor dry. Kennedy and McNally (2005, p. 359), by contrast, treat *the door is not open* as entailing *the door is closed*. In the terminology of §1.7.2, the disagreement concerns whether such antonyms are contraries or contradictories.

Interpreting the truth-value results requires attention to differences between participants as well as adjective classes. In Experiment 2, participants could accept every image that the adjective appropriately described (Xiang et al., 2022, §2.2.1). However, the reanalysis reported in §4.2.6 identifies 23 participants, approximately one fifth of those retained for individual-response analysis, who frequently accepted only the highest-degree image for minimum-standard adjectives and also overwhelmingly did so for relative adjectives (nb appdx. F §11). Their responses contribute a concentration of minimum-standard thresholds at the upper end alongside the concentration just above zero. The resulting group distribution therefore combines markedly different response patterns. One possibility is that these participants understood the task as asking for the best-fitting image; another is that their judgements reflect different thresholds. The present analysis leaves these readings open (§5.3.2), since the response pattern alone does not distinguish them.

This participant variation matters for the antonym question. In the reanalysis, setting these participants aside largely removes the aggregate gap between antonyms for shapes, while a gap remains for artefacts (§4.2.6; nb appdx. F §11). Moreover, Xiang et al.'s (2022, §2.2.1) counterbalancing ensured that no participant judged both antonyms on the same image set. The data therefore permit comparisons between the response distributions for antonyms, but do not directly establish whether an individual participant accepts neither adjective for a given image. These qualifications guide the later use of the class differences and antonym gaps in evaluating the present architecture (§4.2.6) and motivating its proposed extensions (§§5.1.3 and 5.3.2).

## 1.8 Borderline cases: supervaluation, stable atoms, and affirmable assertions

A further question concerns what follows when a statement is not false. Answering it requires distinguishing definite truth from the absence of definite falsity, and successful affirmation from the absence of a refutation. Supervaluation and Vickers's account of assertions judged by finite observations develop these distinctions on different grounds. In supervaluation, a vague predicate has a positive extension, a negative extension and a gap between them. A statement is definitely true if it is true under every way of making the context fully precise (Chierchia, 2010, pp. 118–119). A case in the gap is neither definitely true nor definitely false. Merely failing to establish definite falsity therefore does not yield definite truth. In his account of which nouns combine with numerals, Chierchia (2010, pp. 120–122) uses supervaluation to define stable atomicity. A property's atoms are stable when the same entities remain its atoms in every precisification of the context; otherwise they are unstable. Counting requires stable minimal entities, and numerals presuppose a property with such entities (Chierchia, 2010, pp. 122, 124). Measurement, however, does not impose this stability requirement: if the atoms are vaguely specified, it may be impossible to determine what should be counted while remaining possible to measure the relevant quantity (Chierchia, 2010, p. 118).

Whereas supervaluation considers alternative precisifications, Vickers (1989, pp. 6–8) approaches borderline cases through what finite observations can establish. An assertion is *affirmative* when it is true precisely in the circumstances in which it can be affirmed; the corresponding truth set is open. An assertion is *refutative* when it is false precisely in the circumstances in which it can be refuted; its truth set is closed. Excluding or including boundary cases can therefore make an assertion affirmative or refutative. For example, the assertion that a person is exactly six feet tall can be refuted by a sufficiently discriminating measurement if false, but cannot be affirmed by finite measurement. Failure to refute this assertion is not a successful affirmation of it. This distinction concerns the evidence available for an assertion, alongside the interpretation that assigns its truth conditions.

The role of negation makes the comparison more precise. In supervaluation, negation is classical within each precisification, so a tautology is definitely true even when its component predicate lacks a stable value (Chierchia, 2010, p. 119). Vickers (1989, p. 8) likewise retains classical truth conditions for connectives once the simple assertions have received an interpretation, but separately asks whether the resulting assertion is affirmative or refutative. Affirming the negation of an assertion requires refuting the assertion. Negation thus exchanges affirmative and refutative status and need not preserve affirmability. The contrast concerns stability across precisifications in one account and the availability of finite observational evidence in the other.

## 1.9 Section 1 synthesis

The probabilistic and grammatical accounts reviewed here compute strengthening over represented alternatives but differ in where the computation occurs and which information it uses. Prior-based accounts face empirical questions about both the magnitude and the direction of prior effects. Grammatical exhaustification avoids consulting a prior within the operator, while world knowledge still enters the selection and assessment of readings. Under the modelling assumption that a context set is the support of a Bayesian prior, the finite-world comparison in §§1.4.3–1.5.2 identifies what these representations share and where they differ: they exclude the same propositions before interpretation, but only the prior grades the admitted worlds. Under the conditions stated there, RSA regrades within the literal listener's kernel, while exhaustification changes the truth set and can enlarge this kernel. Neither this comparison nor the empirical record has settled the semantic–pragmatic assignment of scalar implicature, and the assignment itself leaves the processing architecture open.

The review also identifies unresolved questions about the objects over which scalar resolution operates. Alternative sets require an account of cardinality, membership, selection and motivation; scales require an account of their structure and of what makes an expression belong to one. The unlexicalized O corner remains an explanatory problem beyond the proposed tie-break by counting negations. Absolute adjectives raise questions about endpoint-oriented interpretation and about whether antonym pairs leave degrees satisfying neither member. Supervaluation and the finite-observation account, in turn, distinguish ways in which a statement can remain unsettled: absence of definite falsity does not establish definite truth, and absence of refutation does not establish affirmation. §2 introduces the predictive-coding framework in which this paper develops its account of scalar resolution.

# 2. Predictive coding under the free-energy principle: foundations, development, and evidence

## 2.1 The Gaussian density and the Bayesian brain hypothesis

The construction developed below begins with Gaussian assumptions about hidden variables and observations. The univariate Gaussian density is

\[
f(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\},
\qquad \mu\in\mathbb R,\quad \sigma^2>0,
\tag{2.1}
\]

where \(\mu\) is the mean and \(\sigma^2\) the variance. Bogacz (2017, Eq. 2) denotes the variance by \(\Sigma\). The density has support on the whole real line, whereas its parameters range over \(\mathbb R\times\mathbb R_{>0}\). This distinction matters when the quantity being modelled has a narrower physical range: Bogacz (2017, §2) adopts a Gaussian approximation to light intensity for simplicity, while acknowledging the density assigned to negative values.

As a space of distributions, the Gaussian family is a two-parameter exponential family with natural parameters \((\mu/\sigma^2,-1/(2\sigma^2))\) and sufficient statistics \((x,x^2)\). Parameterized by the mean and standard deviation, the family occupies the half-plane \((\mu,\sigma)\), with \(\sigma>0\); the Fisher information metric gives this half-plane constant negative curvature, yielding a hyperbolic geometry (Amari & Nagaoka, 1993/2000, p. vii; Amari, 2016, p. vi). For the construction in §2.2, the relevant algebraic property is that the log-density is quadratic in the difference between a value and its mean. Differentiation therefore yields a linear term weighted by inverse variance.

The Bayesian brain hypothesis treats perception as probabilistic inference: the brain represents uncertainty and combines prior information with a likelihood (Knill & Pouget, 2004; Friston, 2010). The hypothesis leaves the implementation of inference open. As Aitchison and Lengyel (2017) explain, Bayesian inference can use other representations and algorithms, while predictive coding can serve computations other than Bayesian inference. The relation between them must consequently be established for a particular construction. Bogacz's (2017) tutorial provides the construction against which this paper develops its model.

## 2.2 Bogacz's construction in one dimension

Bogacz (2017, §1) begins with two conditions on biological plausibility: local computation and local plasticity. The first restricts the information available to a neuron's computation; the second restricts the information available to a synaptic update. Their definitions and relation to other notions of locality are developed in §2.3. The scalar example shows how an inferential objective can be implemented under these restrictions. Notebook text cell 2 of main.ipynb (hereafter nbtc 2) gives the construction step by step with Bogacz's original equation numbers; the following account develops the steps on which the proposed architecture depends.

An organism must infer the size \(v\) of a food item from an observed light intensity \(u\). The generative model specifies a Gaussian likelihood and prior,

\[
p(u\mid v)=f(u;g(v),\Sigma_u),
\qquad
p(v)=f(v;v_p,\Sigma_p),
\tag{2.2}
\]

where \(g(v)\) predicts the intensity from the size, \(v_p\) is the prior mean, and \(\Sigma_u\) and \(\Sigma_p\) are positive variances (Bogacz, 2017, Eqs. 1–3). Exact Bayesian inference gives

\[
p(v\mid u)=\frac{p(u\mid v)p(v)}{p(u)},
\qquad
p(u)=\int_{\mathbb R}p(u\mid v)p(v)\,dv.
\tag{2.3}
\]

The normalization integral is the obstacle to implementing the exact posterior in the simple network considered by Bogacz (2017, §2.1). For discrete distributions, a circuit for normalization has been proposed in the basal ganglia (Bogacz & Gurney, 2007, as cited in Bogacz, 2017, §2.1). In the scalar construction, Bogacz instead seeks a point estimate \(\phi\). Since \(p(u)\) does not depend on this estimate, maximizing the posterior density at \(\phi\) requires maximizing only the joint density, or its logarithm,

\[
F(\phi)=\log p(\phi)+\log p(u\mid\phi).
\tag{2.4}
\]

The logarithms are natural throughout this construction. Equation (2.4) is Bogacz's Eq. (6); its relation to variational free energy requires a further approximation, considered below. The quadratic Gaussian log-densities make the gradient of Eq. (2.4) a sum of precision-weighted residuals. Defining the prediction errors by

\[
\varepsilon_p=\frac{\phi-v_p}{\Sigma_p},
\qquad
\varepsilon_u=\frac{u-g(\phi)}{\Sigma_u},
\qquad
\dot\phi=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi}
=\varepsilon_u g'(\phi)-\varepsilon_p,
\tag{2.5}
\]

gives Bogacz's (2017) Eqs. (8)–(12), where the dot denotes change over time and \(g'\) is the derivative of the generative mapping. The prior term moves the estimate towards \(v_p\), and the input term adjusts the estimate according to the observed intensity; inverse variances weight their respective influences. Gradient ascent seeks a maximum of \(F\); for a nonlinear \(g\), the estimate may converge to a local maximum. The errors can themselves be obtained through recurrent dynamics,

\[
\dot\varepsilon_p=\phi-v_p-\Sigma_p\varepsilon_p,
\qquad
\dot\varepsilon_u=u-g(\phi)-\Sigma_u\varepsilon_u.
\tag{2.6}
\]

For a fixed estimate, the stationary values of Eq. (2.6) are the errors in Eq. (2.5) (Bogacz, 2017, Eqs. 13–14). In the corresponding network, each node computes from its inputs and connection weights. The prior mean \(v_p\), for example, is stored as the weight of a connection from a tonically active neuron. The estimate and errors are node activities, while the longer-lived parameters are connection strengths. There is no separate read-out stage: the state-unit activities are the represented estimates.

Learning uses the same objective to adjust parameters after observations. For a linear generative mapping \(g(v)=\theta v\), the gradient is \(\partial F/\partial\theta=\varepsilon_u\phi\), a product of an error and a state activity (Bogacz, 2017, Eq. 25). The prior mean and variances also receive updates derived from \(F\), with the variances determining the precision weights in Eq. (2.5). In Bogacz's trial-by-trial learning scheme, differing observations keep the parameters fluctuating; the reference values are those at which the expected update is zero (Bogacz, 2017, §§2.4–2.5). Inference of the current state and learning across observations thus differ in what is adjusted and on what timescale, even though both use gradients of the objective.

The variational formulation explains how one objective can serve both purposes. For an approximating density \(q(v)\) with finite relevant integrals, variational free energy has the two decompositions

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F(q)
&=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(v)\,\|\,p(v)\right)
-\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log p(u\mid v)\right]\\
&=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(v)\,\|\,p(v\mid u)\right)-\log p(u),
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.7}
\]

where \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) is Kullback–Leibler divergence and \(\mathbb E_q\) denotes expectation under \(q\) (nbtc 2; Bogacz, 2017, Eqs. 30–35). Non-negativity of the divergence makes \(\mathcal F\) an upper bound on surprisal, \(-\log p(u)\). For a fixed generative model, minimizing \(\mathcal F\) fits \(q\) to the posterior; varying the generative parameters optimizes the same bound for learning. Bogacz uses the opposite sign, \(F(q)=-\mathcal F(q)\), which lower-bounds \(\log p(u)\) and is maximized. This paper follows his sign convention (nbtc 3, preamble item 4). Prediction error, surprisal and variational free energy therefore name different quantities: a mismatch, the negative log probability density of the observation, and an optimizable bound on this quantity, respectively.

Bogacz (2017, Eq. 34) reduces this variational account to a point estimate by taking \(q\) to be a delta at \(\phi\) and dropping the entropy term \(-\int q\log q\). For a delta, this term is not finite, so the remaining log-joint objective in Eq. (2.4) is not itself the variational bound. The reduction can be understood through a Gaussian approximating density whose width tends to zero: under the smoothness conditions of the scalar example, the bound's gradient in the centre approaches the gradient of the maximum a posteriori objective (nbtc 2). This point-estimate approximation is a separate choice from the Gaussian assumptions in Eq. (2.2). A Laplace approximation can retain both a mode and a covariance (Friston et al., 2007). Moreover, Gaussian prior and likelihood densities can yield a non-Gaussian posterior when the generative mapping is nonlinear, as in Bogacz's \(g(v)=v^2\). The quadratic residual terms explain the subtraction-based errors in this construction; another density family would generally require re-deriving the inference and variance-learning equations.

The construction extends to a hierarchy in which each level predicts the level below (Bogacz, 2017, §4.2). In the vector formulation, a matrix carries predictions downwards and its transpose carries errors upwards; each generative weight is learned from a product of an error and a state activity (Bogacz, 2017, Eqs. 53–56). Covariance learning introduces a further problem, however. The gradient contains the inverse covariance matrix, so updating one connection would require values stored at other connections. Bogacz (2017, §5) replaces this dependence by adding an interneuron to each prediction-error node. Under the assumption that the error subsystem settles much faster than the rest of the network, its stationary activities supply the quantities needed for local covariance learning. Local covariance learning therefore depends on the assumed separation of timescales in addition to the hierarchical organization of the network (Bogacz, 2017, §§5.1–5.2, 6).

## 2.3 From Rao and Ballard to the free-energy principle, and the notions of locality

Rao and Ballard's (1999) hierarchical model of visual cortex provides the canonical predictive-coding architecture. Higher levels send predictions through generative mappings to lower levels, and residual errors return upwards to update estimates of latent states. The network accounts for extra-classical receptive-field effects, including end-stopping. In the free-energy formulation, the ascending errors are weighted by precision. Friston's (2005, 2008, 2010) framework places inference and learning under a common variational objective and extends the account to learned variances and covariances, hierarchical dynamics and action. Attention is modelled through precision weighting (Feldman & Friston, 2010), dynamic generative models represent changing hidden causes (Friston, 2008), and active inference extends the objective to action (Friston et al., 2010). The resulting proposal has the broader ambition of a unified account of brain function (Friston, 2010).

This programme has been influential, but the relation between its formal principles, process models and neural evidence remains contested (Millidge et al., 2021; see §2.4). Bogacz (2017) presents a minimal formulation of Friston's (2005) model, with modifications and extensions that satisfy local computation and local plasticity. This formulation provides the basis for the architecture developed in §3 (nbtc 3, preamble item 4). Understanding the resulting commitments requires distinguishing Bogacz's locality conditions from other restrictions described by the same term.

### 2.3.1 Notions of locality

Locality conditions are motivated both by biological plausibility and by limits on computation. Bogacz's (2017, p. 199) conditions serve the first purpose. *Local computation* permits a neuron to use only the activities of its input neurons and the weights of the synapses carrying these inputs. *Local plasticity* restricts the activity dependence of a synaptic update to the two neurons the synapse connects. Bogacz also allows dependence on that synapse's own strength in the scalar variance-learning rules (2017, §2.4); the violation arises when other synaptic weights are required. Bogacz reports that Rao and Ballard's (1999) model satisfied both conditions, whereas Friston's (2005) model did not. Bogacz's locality conditions thus distinguish constructions within the broader predictive-coding framework.

Other uses of locality limit the information or resources a process must consult. In syntax, subjacency has been derived from a parser with a bounded left context (Berwick & Weinberg, 1984, as cited in Smith, 1986, pp. 224–225). Chomsky (2008, pp. 138, 143) relates the Phase Impenetrability Condition to minimal computation and binary Merge to restricted computational resources. In cognitive architecture, informationally encapsulated modules gain speed by consulting limited information (Fodor, 1983, p. 70), while the subsystems of a nearly decomposable hierarchy are approximately independent over short timescales (Simon, 1962, p. 474). Probabilistic propagation similarly distributes inference among processors that exchange messages with their neighbours (Pearl, 1988, pp. 145–146; Lauritzen & Spiegelhalter, 1988). A related requirement is that cognitive models be tractable (I. van Rooij, 2008); in predictive processing, tractability depends in part on the number of competing hypotheses at each level (Kwisthout & van Rooij, 2020). These proposals share a concern with computational demands while restricting different aspects of processing.

### 2.3.2 The notions compared in form

The distinctions become clearer when the conditions are classified by what they restrict. Bogacz's local computation and Pearl's propagation constrain the information available to a unit's update, whereas local plasticity constrains the information available to a parameter update. Encapsulation and near-decomposability concern connections or effective dependencies between subsystems. Subjacency and phase impenetrability restrict access to portions of a derivation or processing history, while binary Merge restricts the number of objects combined in one operation. Tractability concerns the difficulty of computing the function. These are distinct restrictions on access, organization and computational cost; satisfying one does not establish that the others are satisfied.

Form and motivation do not coincide. Local computation and message propagation impose a similar kind of access restriction for different reasons, while proposals motivated by bounded computation take several forms. Even the two access restrictions differ in scope: Bogacz's neuron may read only its own input activities and synaptic weights, whereas Pearl's (1988, p. 145) processors may also inspect parameters maintained by neighbouring processors. Among the conditions considered here, local plasticity specifically restricts learning. This distinction matters because an inference scheme can use local messages while its parameter updates require information collected from elsewhere.

Bogacz's two constraints identify three recurrent violations. A computation may require a parameter stored elsewhere: in Friston's (2005) formulation, an error can be scaled by a parameter held on other connections (Bogacz, 2017, p. 209). The weight-transport problem raises a related issue. The predictive-coding formulation that approximates backpropagation through local Hebbian plasticity requires symmetric forward and backward weights (Whittington & Bogacz, 2017, p. 1254), as does standard backpropagation (Lillicrap et al., 2016, p. 2); later work relaxes this requirement (Millidge et al., 2020). A second violation occurs when updating one parameter requires another parameter, as in the covariance inverse removed by Bogacz's (2017, p. 207) modified network. A third occurs when one shared parameter receives update contributions from several sites, the case considered in §2.3.3. These dependencies violate the specified unit or synapse access conditions; they do not by themselves establish a violation of the other restrictions discussed above.[^otherforms]

### 2.3.3 Weight sharing

Weight sharing raises two distinct objections to biological implementation. Updating a shared weight requires contributions from every position at which the weight applies, extending the update's information requirements beyond the activities at a single connection (Millidge et al., 2022, Appendix A; Pogodin et al., 2021, p. 1). Independently of the learning rule, maintaining identical connection strengths across physically separated sites is itself difficult (Ott et al., 2020). The first objection concerns how a parameter is updated; the second concerns how equal values are realized and maintained.

Several biological phenomena provide relevant comparisons, although their mechanisms differ from the pooled update just described. Development applies common rules at many sites: fifteen columnar cell types recur in nearly every column of the fly medulla (Nern et al., 2025, p. 1226), and genomic specification must compress a wiring diagram into developmental rules (Zador, 2019, p. 1). A common factor can also influence many synapses from one source, as in synaptic scaling (Turrigiano et al., 1998), gain modulation (Salinas & Thier, 2000), or the neuromodulatory signal in three-factor learning rules (Frémaux & Gerstner, 2016, p. 8). Such factors depend on activity levels or modulatory input rather than a Hebbian product pooled over all affected connections. Other models obtain the function of weight sharing with separate weights. Training on translated inputs can make untied weights approximately equal (Ott et al., 2020), and lateral connections combined with Hebbian plasticity and a sleep-like phase can equalize untied weights through local mechanisms (Pogodin et al., 2021). Common development, common modulation and local equalization consequently provide distinct ways of coordinating connections. Learning one shared parameter from products formed at multiple sites would require an additional mechanism for collecting these products. Evidence that one source influences many connections establishes the opposite direction of dependence. The locality departure in the present architecture concerns precisely this pooling of contributions to a shared learning update (§3.4).

## 2.4 Challenges to the framework

Predictive coding and the free-energy principle offer a framework for relating inference, learning and neural computation, but their status as descriptions of cortical processing remains contested. Critics ask whether the free-energy principle is an empirically falsifiable theory or a general modelling framework (Colombo & Wright, 2021; Gershman, 2019), and whether predictive-coding proposals are specified sufficiently to support discriminating tests (Kogo & Trengove, 2015). The distinction between an algorithmic signal and a neural implementation is apparent in the negative-firing-rate problem. Gaussian subtraction yields signed errors, whereas a spiking neuron cannot have a negative firing rate. Separate populations for the two signs or alternative normalization schemes are proposed ways of addressing this discrepancy. Circuit accounts associate feedforward and feedback pathways with errors and predictions (Bastos et al., 2012; Keller & Mrsic-Flogel, 2018), but identifying the computations represented by observed signals remains an empirical problem (Spratling, 2013; Furutachi & Hofer, 2026). In language, Lewis and Bastiaansen (2015, p. 155) propose a predictive-coding account of beta and gamma oscillations during sentence comprehension.

Connectomics provides increasingly detailed structural evidence. Synapse-resolved reconstructions cover the whole adult fly brain (Schlegel et al., 2024), a cubic millimetre of mouse visual cortex with functional recordings (MICrONS Consortium, 2025), and a cubic millimetre of human temporal cortex without corresponding functional recordings (Shapson-Coe et al., 2024). Applying these methods to scalar resolution would require identifying the relevant language circuitry and the computations represented by neural activity. Functional evidence would remain necessary even with complete structural coverage, since a wiring diagram underdetermines the supported computation (Bargmann & Marder, 2013). Extending the proposal to neural circuits therefore calls for further empirical work and more detailed specifications linking the model's quantities to neural activity (§0). At the level of the model itself, the free-energy principle also leaves lexical semantics, scalar alternatives and communicative goals to be supplied by the linguistic application.

Precision learning presents a different challenge. Precision weights control the influence of priors and prediction errors, and the construction in §2.2 shows how variance parameters can be learned. If precision and its assignment to levels or populations remain unconstrained, however, a model may accommodate an observation after the fact. The concern therefore centres on unconstrained precision assignments and the resulting difficulty of deriving discriminating predictions (Litwin & Miłkowski, 2020; Colombo & Wright, 2021; Walsh et al., 2020). Gaussianity and locality likewise specify assumptions whose empirical adequacy must be assessed for the cortical representations and learning processes being modelled. Three-factor learning rules and factors that modulate a whole unit (§2.3.3) show that biological plasticity is not confined to strictly two-factor rules. A broadcast signal can nevertheless be physically available at each receiving synapse. Such evidence challenges the universality of Bogacz's two-factor condition while leaving open whether biological learning satisfies a different locality condition.

## 2.5 Elicitation, falsifiability, and stipulations

The available evidence for scalar resolution includes offline truth-value judgements and slider ratings (Degen et al., 2015; Xiang et al., 2022), as well as online measures such as response times (Bott & Noveck, 2004), visual-world eye-tracking (Huang & Snedeker, 2009), event-related potentials (Nieuwland et al., 2010) and mouse-tracking (Tomlinson et al., 2013). An elicited response reflects an inferential state through the decision and response processes operating at the time of measurement. Recovering the preceding trajectory, or determining whether inference had already settled, requires assumptions about these processes. Franke et al. (2016), for example, model prior elicitation through task-specific link functions from latent beliefs to observable responses. Elicited outcomes can therefore bear on the order of computation, the second question of §0, when competing process accounts predict different responses under specified assumptions linking inference to behaviour.

For priors, three objects must be distinguished: a probability explicitly supplied to an observer, a prior belief the observer reports, and the prior used during interpretation. Bévalot and Meyniel (2024) found that explicitly supplied priors were weighted less than implicitly learned priors and entered different computations in perceptual inference. Thakur et al. (2021) compared learners with and without explicit awareness of learned statistical regularities and found different mechanisms for implementing perceptual bias. Probabilities also receive different weights when described and when experienced (Hertwig et al., 2004; Wu et al., 2009). Relatedly, reported confidence has been modelled as a second-order read-out of a first-order state (Fleming & Daw, 2017). These distinctions motivate investigating how reported beliefs relate to the beliefs used during scalar interpretation; whether the corresponding priors differ remains an empirical question.

Variation across elicitation tasks also requires interpretation. The prior reported for the same set of items can change with the task (§4.1.1), but a difference in responses need not imply a difference in beliefs. Franke et al. (2016) model slider ratings over bins, a single-number response and choices between two bins as generated from one latent belief per participant and item. Their results suggest broad consistency across the three measures under the proposed link functions: single-number responses tend towards modal values, and mean slider ratings approximate the inferred population-level belief. The claim is conditional on this response model, which leaves some response patterns unexplained. Thus, agreement between measures and discrepancies between them both have to be assessed through the assumptions linking beliefs to responses.

Xiang et al. (2022, §2.1.2) raise this issue for the priors used in the adjective study reviewed in §1.7.3. Their task asks participants to select the most likely image and pools the single choices into a prior distribution. The authors acknowledge the unnatural wording of the prompt and the possibility that the aggregate corresponds to no individual's prior, comparing the procedure to Franke et al.'s (2016) single-number task. A further experiment with sliders on the same image sets produced largely uniform priors. Xiang et al. leave the validity of elicitation methods across contexts to future work while retaining confidence in their original task. The studies considered here do not test whether an offline prior report differs from the prior operative during online interpretation of the same items. This paper therefore reserves judgement on the correspondence between reported and operative priors.

These observational limits also bear on what a successful model fit establishes. Falsifiability requires the possibility of conflict with observation (Popper, 1959/2002, p. 18), but observations can underdetermine a theory (Quine, 1951). Anderson's (1978, p. 263) representation–process mimicry makes the problem concrete: different combinations of representations and processes can predict the same behavioural record. A good fit must therefore be assessed against what alternative accounts could predict and how flexibly the model can accommodate data (Roberts & Pashler, 2000; Pitt & Myung, 2002). The flexibility of Bayesian cognitive models makes this concern particularly relevant here (Jones & Love, 2011; Bowers & Davis, 2012). The idea of a crucial experiment assumes that an observation can single out an account among its rivals (Bacon, 1620/1902, Book II, aphorism 36), an assumption rejected by Duhem (1954, p. 188, as cited in Popper, 1959/2002, p. 58). Where several accounts predict the same observation, this observation cannot uniquely identify one of them.

This paper rejects the uniqueness assumption for the elicited outcomes under consideration. Nevertheless, empirical comparison remains possible: observations can reject an account or favour the account over a rival with less successful predictions. Identifiability is relative to specified observations and to the auxiliary assumptions connecting these observations to the account. For elicited priors, the suspended question above is already sufficient to prevent the report from uniquely fixing which prior an inference used. Inquiry remains constrained by what can be observed; a demand for empirical discrimination beyond this limit supplies no usable test. The adoption of Marr's levels in §0 responds to the same limitation. Stipulations are consequently treated as auxiliary hypotheses, in Lakatos's (1970) sense of a protective belt around a programme's hard core. They are declared, varied where variation is possible, and retained explicitly in statements of the results that depend on them. This treatment allows an assessment of what the architecture explains under the declared commitments and where an alternative choice would require a new comparison. Representational appropriateness is judged relative to these declared stipulations.

[^otherforms]: Pearl's (1988, p. 145) processors may inspect neighbouring processors' parameters, and the propagation account considered here concerns inference with fixed parameters. Simon's (1962, p. 474) nearly decomposable systems can depend on other subsystems in aggregate over longer timescales. Summing contributions from two units respects a restriction to binary operations. A finite sum or a matrix inversion does not by itself establish intractability; computational cost still depends on the representation and input size.

# 3. Proposed architecture

## 3.0 Design strategy: determinacy

The architecture selects among the choices left open by Bogacz's construction according to a methodological preference for determinacy. A configuration should fix a reporting target against which the recurrent dynamics can be checked, without making this target depend on initialization, integration path or an arbitrary stopping tolerance. The empirical adequacy of the selected representation of scalar resolution remains a separate question: results are conditional on the choices defining each configuration, and alternative representations require future comparison. Following the notebook's notation, this section writes \(\mathcal F\) for the architecture's **maximized** objective, with Bogacz's sign convention; this reverses the sign attached to the conventional variational \(\mathcal F\) in Eq. (2.7) (nbtc 3, preamble item 4).

Three choices implement the strategy. First, affine generative mappings and Gaussian densities make the objective a strictly concave quadratic in the state when the plastic gain \(\theta_u\), introduced in §3.3, is fixed. The objective tends to negative infinity as the state norm grows, so its negative is coercive. There is consequently a unique maximizer, obtainable by solving the linear stationarity equations (nbtc 3 §§6, 8.1). Second, the situation level carries a field over world states, preserving the affine mappings on which this result depends (§3.2). Third, predictions are reported at the asymptotic gain \(\theta_u^\ast\), a value fixed by the configuration rather than by the tolerance used to halt a numerical trajectory (nbtc 3 §8.5; nb appdx. B, Eq. B2). Reaching this target is conditional on the learning protocol: the gain starts at \(\theta_u(0)=0\), the stipulated exposure ensemble is used, and the fast subsystem equilibrates between updates (§3.4). The objective is not jointly concave in the gain and utility state, and other starts need not approach the reported asymptote.

Exact Bayesian computation would be possible in a sufficiently small state space. The variational formulation is adopted here to specify recurrent dynamics whose implementation properties can be tested against closed-form targets (nbtc 3 §8.6). The same design choices impose computational costs: a field representation requires a value at every grid node. Moreover, the dynamics become stiffer as \(\theta_u^2\) grows, tightening the separation of timescales required by the architecture (nbtc 3 §8.3). The learning flow has no principled halting rule, which motivates reporting its asymptote rather than treating a tolerance-dependent stopping value as the prediction (§3.4; nbtc 3 §8.5).

## 3.1 Design commitments

The commitments fall into two groups: requirements inherited from the adopted construction and choices stipulated by this paper where the construction permits alternatives. Both are stipulations about the process being modelled (§2.5). Distinguishing the two groups identifies which choices can be varied while retaining the adopted construction and which changes would require a different construction. Interpretation is listener-side model inversion: the utterance is observed, while the world state is inferred through the network's representations. The recurrent interaction involved differs from RSA's recursive reasoning about speakers and listeners (§1.2). RSA requires a choice of recursion depth, and this recursion can be continued without bound. The present architecture instead computes interpretation through recurrent settling under a single objective (§3.4). This choice avoids open-ended recursion over agents, while the duration and halting of the recurrent dynamics remain separate questions.

The construction supplies a common objective for inference and learning. State dynamics implement ascent on \(\mathcal F\) in the instantaneous-error limit, while the plastic gain follows a gradient of the same objective (§2.2; nbtc 3 §§5, 7–8). State optimization at a fixed gain and learning of this gain are analysed separately (§3.4). Local computation is also imposed before the architecture is built, determining which messages an update may use in Bogacz's sense (§2.3.1). The construction also imposes local plasticity; however, the shared-gain update in the present architecture violates this constraint at the implemented utility dimension, \(m=2\). At \(m=1\), the learning rule has local Hebbian form under the projection-gain interpretation specified in §3.3 (cf. Whittington & Bogacz, 2017). The two-dimensional departure is examined in §3.4, and §5.2.4 proposes a route to an implementation with one utility dimension per level. The Gaussian assumptions concern the real-valued field amplitudes and utility coordinates, whereas the domain used to label field components is specified separately (§3.2).

Several further choices narrow the architecture. World knowledge is allowed to constrain interpretation without lexical meaning becoming indefeasible, a commitment motivated by §1.3 and implemented through finite lexical strength (§3.2). A closed-form stationary point is required so that the recurrent dynamics can be tested against an independently specified target (§3.0). Every variance is fixed at one, and this phase performs no precision inference (nbtc 3 §3, item 1).[^sigma] Fixing the variances removes one source of post hoc flexibility; the remaining representational choices still require assessment in light of the broader concern discussed in §2.4. The following subsections develop these choices: the continuous scale, log-odds coordinate, field representation and exclusion coding in §3.2; the terminating utility level, projection gain and coupling condition in §3.3; and the two-dimensional utility basis in §3.5.

## 3.2 Continuous scale, the base world prior, and the soft lexical field

The hidden state consists of a situation field \(\varphi_S\in\mathbb R^K\) and utility coordinates \(\varphi_u\in\mathbb R^m\), where \(K\) is the number of grid nodes and \(m\) the utility dimension. The construction's Gaussian densities are over these state variables (nbtc 3 §5, Eq. 13). The proportion \(s\) labels the field's components, so the domain of \(s\) is specified separately: the architecture stipulates a continuous world scale so that quantifiers and gradable adjectives can be represented within one framework (§1.7). For a predicate resolving \(n\) counted atoms, the \(n+1\) possible proportions are points on this continuous scale (nb appdx. A).

Every lexical entry is defined on the complete scale \([0,1]\), with *no* and *all* anchored at its endpoints (nbtc 3 §3, item 2). The interval is a totally closed scale in Kennedy and McNally's (2005, p. 354) typology; the dimension measured by a predicate corresponds here to the world quantity represented by a configuration, the proportion \(s\). Closing the scale bounds the range of world values over which interpretation is represented and supplies two anchors for the entries. The coordinate used to represent its interior is a further stipulation:

\[
\zeta=\operatorname{logit}(s)=\log\frac{s}{1-s},
\qquad
s=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\zeta}},
\qquad s\in(0,1),\quad\zeta\in\mathbb R.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Although the complete scale includes both endpoints, the logit coordinate represents only the open interval, on which the map is bijective. The grid is uniform in \(\zeta\), the lexical threshold \(\theta_L\) acts in \(\zeta\), and the mode criteria are evaluated over \(\zeta\) (§§3.3, 3.6). Two properties motivate this choice. Translation by \(\theta_L\) multiplies the odds by \(e^{\theta_L}\), giving the threshold the interpretation of a log-gain (nb appdx. A). In addition, the density of a Beta base world prior expressed in \(\zeta\), including the change-of-variable factor, has an interior mode for positive Beta shape parameters. The evaluation criteria require this interior mode (nbtc 3 §1).

Integrals over the coordinate are approximated on a quadrature grid with nodes \(\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_K\), truncated at \(\pm Z\), and positive weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_K\). Writing \(W=\operatorname{diag}(w_1,\ldots,w_K)\), the field norm is

\[
\lVert f\rVert_W^2=f^{\mathsf T}Wf
=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_kf_k^2,
\qquad f\in\mathbb R^K,\quad w_k>0.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Discretization applies to the field's domain, while the amplitudes at the grid nodes remain continuous and unbounded. The node count \(K\) controls numerical accuracy, and the settled fields converge as \(K\) is refined at a fixed half-width. By contrast, the half-width \(Z\) is a stipulated truncation of the represented coordinate. Changing \(Z\) changes the quadrature on which the utility basis is constructed, so the architecture has no analogous convergence target under variation of the half-width (nbtc 3 §1).

The field representation is chosen for three reasons. A single-unit encoding that introduces the lexical region through a nonlinear map loses the concavity used for the unique stationary target; other scalar encodings are not examined. The evaluation also asks where belief lies on the scale, including how much mass falls in specified regions, and the utility level acts on the belief's shape. A single point estimate supplies neither this mass distribution nor a width (nbtc 3 §1). The situation units therefore carry log-weights at the grid nodes, while \(s\) appears as a unit's label.[^field] This choice preserves affine mappings at the cost of representing the field value by value, as anticipated in §3.0.

For an utterance \(y\), let \(E_y\) be the set of world states excluded by its lexical entry, and let \(\chi_y\) be the indicator of the corresponding excluded region on the grid. The realization of endpoint entries on the grid uses the threshold defined in §3.3 (nbtc 3 §3, item 2). Exclusion coding is chosen for its treatment of a tautological entry, which maps to the zero field: the absence of lexical information thus corresponds to the absence of a lexical contribution (nb appdx. D §3). Let \(\Lambda>0\) denote lexical strength, \(p_0\) the base world prior over the coordinate, and \(\ell_{0,k}=\log p_0(\zeta_k)\) its log sampled on the grid. The lexical field and its prediction from the situation level are

\[
\varphi_L=\Lambda\chi_y,
\qquad
\ell_0=\log p_0,
\qquad
 g_L(\varphi_S)=\ell_0-\varphi_S.
\tag{3.3}
\]

Here the logarithm of \(p_0\) is understood node by node. The placement of \(\ell_0\) as the constant term of \(g_L\) is stipulated (nbtc 3 §4, Eq. 9).[^ell0] At each grid node, the lexical residual combines the corresponding components of \(\ell_0\) and \(\varphi_L\) with that node's situation activity. The competing contributions of the base world prior and lexical strength are therefore available at the same error unit. Lexical strength is finite: this **override stipulation** makes the entry defeasible, so a sufficiently concentrated base world prior can override its penalty (nbtc 3 §3, item 6; nbtc 6). The field \(\ell_0-\Lambda\chi_y\) is a softened version of \(\log L_0\), up to normalization; increasing \(\Lambda\) without bound recovers a hard truth-conditional restriction (nbtc 4, Part B). The placement in Eq. (3.3) also provides a literal-listener control. Disconnecting utility by setting \(\theta_u=0\) and removing the situation level's own error contribution through \(\sigma_S\to\infty\) yields the literal-listener field. Disconnecting utility alone, while retaining unit variances, instead yields the tempered field \(\tfrac12(\ell_0-\varphi_L)\) (nbtc 3 §7; nb appdx. D §5). Recovering the untempered literal listener therefore requires both manipulations. The infinite-variance limit defines an algebraic control, whereas the implemented architecture retains unit variances.

The base world prior and the Gaussian prior at the situation level concern different domains. The situation prior is a distribution over field configurations centred on the utility prediction \(g_S(\varphi_u)\). The variational field \(\varphi_S\) settles jointly with \(\varphi_u\), while \(\ell_0\) enters the prediction of the lexical level through \(g_L\) (nbtc 3 §4, Eqs. 7 and 11). Thus, the Gaussian assumptions do not require a Gaussian \(p_0\), and the evaluation includes non-Gaussian base world priors (§4.1.4).[^gauss] The requirement is that \(p_0\) be positive at every represented node, so that \(\ell_0\) is finite there. Where no elicited base world prior is supplied, the default is \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) in \(\zeta\), chosen for its symmetry about the scale's midpoint, unimodality in this coordinate and positivity at every node (nbtc 3 §3, item 4).[^neglight]

## 3.3 Prediction chain and lexical threshold

The levels form the prediction chain

\[
y\leftarrow\varphi_L\leftarrow\varphi_S\leftarrow\varphi_u\leftarrow1.
\tag{3.4}
\]

Each level predicts the one to its left; the final \(1\) denotes a tonically active unit. The choice of levels, including utility as the terminating level, is stipulated (nbtc 3, preamble item 1; §4, Eq. 7). The observed utterance clamps the lexical field, keeping the representation supplied by the entry fixed within this phase. A fixed decoder \(A\) can be calibrated so that its predicted word form equals the observed word form at each clamp. The utterance-level error is then zero as a consequence of the clamp together with this decoder (nbtc 3 §4, Eq. 8; nb appdx. A, Eq. A2).

With the lexical clamp held fixed, zero utterance error also makes the threshold-gain gradient in nb appdx. A, Eq. (A4), vanish. A separate specification is therefore needed to fix the threshold's value. This phase stipulates a relation between \(\theta_L\) and the resolution \(n\), where \(n\) denotes the number of atoms resolved by a counted predicate or the number of distinguishable steps afforded by an uncounted scale. For equally spaced counts, the proportions \(k/n\), with \(k=0,\ldots,n\), divide the scale into Voronoi cells, each containing the proportions nearest to one count. The chosen relation makes the region left open by *all* the cell of count \(n\), and the region left open by *no* the cell of count \(0\). The boundary lies half a step from each endpoint, which fixes

\[
\frac{1}{1+e^{\theta_L}}=\frac{1}{2n}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\theta_L=\log(2n-1).
\tag{3.5}
\]

The uniqueness of the formula follows from the equal-spacing and endpoint-cell requirements; these requirements are stipulated (nb appdx. A, Eq. A5). The relation connects the number of atoms a predicate resolves to the threshold used for a gradable scale. The default \(n=10\) is also stipulated, while what determines \(n\) remains open. §4.1.7 examines the dependence of scalar implicature on \(n\), and §5.3.1 proposes a later account of learning \(\theta_L\). Two limits at the endpoints must be distinguished. Exact zero and one lack logit coordinates regardless of \(n\). Separately, at finite resolution, *no* leaves open a cell of width \(1/(2n)\), within which the architecture does not distinguish exact zero from the other proportions; *all* has the corresponding cell at the opposite end. This indistinguishability follows from the endpoint-cell stipulation. If humans use this architecture, then the same resolution limit applies to their representation of absolute zero (nb appdx. A). The implemented endpoints are symmetric because one \(\theta_L\) determines both cells. An alternatives level that fixes the two ends separately need not preserve this symmetry (§5.2.5).

Utility predicts the situation field through

\[
g_S(\varphi_u)=\theta_u B\varphi_u,
\qquad \theta_u\in\mathbb R,
\qquad B\in\mathbb R^{K\times m}.
\tag{3.6}
\]

The fixed profile \(B\) and its single plastic gain \(\theta_u\) are stipulated; the base world prior does not enter this map (nbtc 3 §4, item 3). Bogacz's (2017, Eq. 56) interlevel weights form a free matrix whose entries each receive a Hebbian update. In the present architecture, the interlevel weights are instead tied to a single scalar gain. At \(m=1\), locality depends on interpreting \(B\) as the fixed profile of one projection, with the gain applied at the utility unit to both the unit's outgoing activity and the incoming error pooled through this profile. Interpreting locality at the projection level extends Bogacz's synaptic plasticity condition and constitutes an additional commitment of the present architecture (nb appdx. B, *Locality*). At \(m=2\), the gain update requires contributions from two utility units and is not local (§3.4).

The reciprocal mappings use \(B\) for descending predictions and \(B^{\mathsf T}W\) for ascending error, both under \(\theta_u\) (nbtc 3 §7, Eq. 19). Since \(B\) is fixed, its reciprocity requires no transport of a learned profile; at one utility dimension, the gain can be applied at the same unit in both directions. The basis is chosen to satisfy \(B^{\mathsf T}WB=I_m\), where \(I_m\) is the identity on the utility coordinates, and \(B^{\mathsf T}W\mathbf1_K=0\), where \(\mathbf1_K\) is the field of ones. These conditions make the columns of \(B\) orthonormal under the quadrature and orthogonal to the constant field. The orthonormality gives the learning analysis its scalar form (nb appdx. B, Eqs. B1–B2; nb appdx. D).

The utility state is anchored by a terminating Gaussian prior with fixed mean \(\mu_u\in\mathbb R^m\); the stipulated value is \(\mu_u=\mathbf1_m\), the vector of ones.[^muu] With all variances equal to one and the zero utterance-error term omitted, the maximized objective is

\[
\mathcal F=-\frac12\lVert\varphi_S-(\ell_0-\varphi_L)\rVert_W^2
-\frac12\lVert\varphi_S-\theta_uB\varphi_u\rVert_W^2
-\frac12\lVert\varphi_u-\mu_u\rVert^2+C,
\tag{3.7}
\]

where the final norm is the ordinary Euclidean norm and \(C\) collects terms constant in the state and plastic gain (nbtc 3 §5, Eq. 13). The first two terms penalize discrepancies at the lexical and situation levels, while the third anchors the utility state. Their affine residuals, at a fixed gain, give the strict concavity used in §3.0. For each utterance, the utility projection of the softened literal-listener field is

\[
c_y=B^{\mathsf T}W(\ell_0-\varphi_L).
\tag{3.8}
\]

Since \(B^{\mathsf T}W\mathbf1_K=0\), this projection discards the field's constant component (nb appdx. C). The projection is consequently invariant to constant shifts of the field, whereas the full architecture remains sensitive to the constant component (nb appdx. D). The reported learning optimum is established for configurations with \(\mu_u^{\mathsf T}\sum_y c_y\neq0\), where the sum runs over the exposure inventory specified in §3.4 and the pairing is Euclidean in utility space. The nonzero-coupling condition is stronger than \(\mu_u\neq0\); nb appdx. B analyses the learning flows when the coupling vanishes. Note that the restriction is specific to the reported optimum of this architecture, whereas Bogacz's construction retains a defined learning rule at prior mean \(v_p=0\), even though his linear example has no finite optimum at this value (Bogacz, 2017, §2.5, Eq. 25).

[^sigma]: Bogacz (2017) learns variances through the rules of §2.4, Eqs. (20)–(21), and introduces an interneuron construction for local covariance learning in §5. Fixing variances here removes this learning component.

[^field]: In Bogacz's (2017, §2.2) examples, a state unit estimates the world quantity itself, and its settled activity is an estimate of this quantity's most likely value. Here the situation units instead represent field amplitudes.

[^ell0]: Structurally, each component of \(\ell_0\) is a weight from a tonic unit, as Bogacz's prior mean \(v_p\) is. The components of \(\ell_0\) are fixed, whereas Bogacz learns \(v_p\) (Bogacz, 2017, §§2.3–2.4, Eq. 19).

[^gauss]: Bogacz's (2017, §2, Eqs. 1–3) prior over his hidden world quantity is Gaussian. Here the world coordinate labels the field, so the Gaussian densities over state amplitudes do not require a Gaussian base world prior (§3.2).

[^muu]: Bogacz learns his prior mean (2017, §2.4, Eq. 19); the terminating mean \(\mu_u\) is fixed in the present architecture.

## 3.4 Dynamics, convergence, learning, and locality

The architecture operates on three timescales. Error units relax towards the residuals associated with their levels, situation and utility units change in response to these errors, and the gain changes through slower learning. When error relaxation is instantaneous, the state updates are gradient ascent on \(\mathcal F\). With finite relaxation times, however, error activity can lag behind the current residual, so the state trajectory need not follow the instantaneous gradient (nbtc 3 §§7–8.2). To state the separation assumed here, let \(\tau_\varepsilon\), \(\tau_\varphi\) and \(\tau_\theta\) be the positive time constants of errors, states and learning. For the combined state \(x=(\varphi_S,\varphi_u)\), the metric \(G=\operatorname{diag}(W,I_m)\) combines the quadrature-weighted inner product for the field with the Euclidean inner product for utility. Write \(H=-G^{-1}\partial^2\mathcal F/\partial x^2\) for the negative Hessian in this metric and \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\) for its largest eigenvalue, evaluated at the current gain. The architecture assumes

\[
\tau_\varepsilon\leq\frac{\tau_\varphi}{4\lambda_{\max}(H)}
\ll\tau_\varphi\ll\tau_\theta.
\tag{3.9}
\]

The critical-damping bound in Eq. (3.9) is a quantitative commitment of this architecture (nbtc 3 §8.3).[^timescale] The bound prevents oscillatory modes in the fixed-gain dynamics and tightens as \(\theta_u^2\) grows. Convergence requires weaker conditions: at a fixed gain, the coupled state and error system converges for every positive \(\tau_\varepsilon\) and \(\tau_\varphi\), even without the separation in Eq. (3.9). Convergence and non-oscillatory relaxation must also be distinguished from monotonic ascent. Empirically, \(\mathcal F\) did not decrease in the inferences audited under the bound and the implemented start, with errors initially zero, the situation field at \(\ell_0\), and utility at \(\mu_u\). The monotonicity finding is therefore specific to the audited trajectories and their initial conditions (nbtc 3 §8.2). At the slow end of the ordering, \(\tau_\varphi/\tau_\theta\) supplies the relative learning rate. The continuous-time architecture introduces no separate learning step size; numerical integration discretizes its dynamics (nbtc 3 §7).

At fixed \(\theta_u\), strict concavity holds for every lexical strength, fixed basis and utility dimension. The closed-form stationary state is therefore the unique global maximizer to which the coupled dynamics converge (nbtc 3 §§8.1–8.4, 8.6). Changing the lexical field shifts the maximizer while leaving the Hessian unchanged, so lexical shape does not affect fixed-gain stability. Entries nevertheless matter for the cost of inference through learning: they help determine \(\theta_u^\ast\), whose magnitude affects stiffness. A unique equilibrium can therefore coexist with substantial variation in the computational cost of reaching this equilibrium. Within these dynamics, descending predictions and ascending errors support joint settlement: given the clamped lexical field, \(\varphi_S\) and \(\varphi_u\) are inferred together. Joint settlement characterizes the computation specified here; a staged algorithm could reach the same unique solution without implementing this process.

Learning begins at the stipulated value \(\theta_u(0)=0\). With utility disconnected, the equilibrated situation field is the tempered control \(\tfrac12(\ell_0-\varphi_L)\), so the subsequent effect of utility can be measured as a displacement from a state encountered by the learning process. After the fast subsystem has equilibrated, the gain follows the objective's Hebbian gradient, with results reported at \(\theta_u^\ast\) under the nonzero-coupling condition of §3.3 (nbtc 3 §7; nb appdx. B).[^converge] This asymptote depends on exposure. Because exposure frequencies are not modelled in this phase, every utterance receives equal weight. The choice parallels the use of unit variances in the absence of precision inference. Non-uniform exposure can change the asymptote; the size of this change is not evaluated.

The exposure inventory is also stipulated to contain at least one entry together with its kernel. On the grid, the notation means \(\ker\chi=\mathbf1_K-\chi\), the indicator of the complementary region (nb appdx. D §1). Thus, the partner is defined by the representation, without assuming a lexical relation of antonymy. The quantifier inventory contains the pair *some* and *no*, together with *all*, whose kernel is absent. Under the alternatives level proposed in §5.2.2, each level would instead contain exactly one complementary pair. The sum over utterances in the analysis of \(\theta_u^\ast\) describes accumulation across exposure, whereas a single gain update requires pooling across utility units (nb appdx. B, *Locality*). The gain update shows why pooling across utility units raises a locality problem. Let \(b_j\) be column \(j\) of \(B\), and let \(\varepsilon_S\) be the situation-error field. At equilibration, \(\varepsilon_S=\varphi_S-\theta_uB\varphi_u\) under the unit variances, and the update for one presented utterance is

\[
\tau_\theta\dot\theta_u
=\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial\theta_u}
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varphi_{u,j}\,b_j^{\mathsf T}W\varepsilon_S.
\tag{3.10}
\]

Each product combines a utility activity with the error pooled through its own profile. At \(m=1\), both factors are available at the single utility unit under the projection-gain interpretation of §3.3. Unit-wide multiplicative factors provide a precedent for the gain arrangement (§2.3.3), although the cited mechanisms do not establish the proposed Hebbian learning rule. At the implemented \(m=2\), however, the shared gain requires the sum of products formed at two sites, and neither unit receives this sum. The gain update consequently violates local plasticity through the weight-sharing mechanism discussed in §2.3.3: the required information spans distinct sites.[^sharedgain]

The use of one scalar gain has a second motivation, beyond its interpretation as a single projection. A free matrix of interlevel weights would allow local Hebbian updates, as in Bogacz's construction, but would introduce an unbounded direction of improvement under the conditions analysed in nb appdx. B. With more than one utility dimension, the terminating mean anchors one direction. Components of settled utility orthogonal to this direction can be contracted towards the mean while compensating by enlarging the corresponding matrix weights, preserving predictions and reducing the terminating-prior penalty. Unless every settled utility state lies along the mean's direction, this rescaling precludes a finite maximizer. Restricting the weights to \(\theta_u B\) removes this rescaling freedom and permits the determinate gain target of §3.3. The restriction thus serves the reporting strategy while creating the shared-gain locality problem at two utility dimensions.

A descending relay representing \(B\varphi_u\) in \(K\) units would relocate the summation used to form predictions. However, the gain update would still require the same aggregate of error–activity products across units (nb appdx. B, *Locality*).[^relay] The relay would therefore leave the present architecture's locality violation unresolved. The proposal in §5.2.4 offers a possible remedy by assigning one opposition and one utility dimension to each level. The current use of two dimensions at a single level serves a representational purpose, developed in §3.5.

## 3.5 Utility basis: tilt and width

The basis has two dimensions so that tilt and width can vary independently (nbtc 5, Part A). The odd column is a monotone ramp whose contribution to the log-field favours one endpoint over the other. The even column raises both tails relative to the centre and supplies the width coordinate. The coefficient determines the direction of the even column's contribution; the resulting change in normalized variance or preference for central versus extreme interpretations also depends on the rest of the field. Two dimensions are a stipulation supported by a restricted separation result. On a grid symmetric about \(\zeta=0\), a basis function has definite parity when the function is either even or odd. An odd function cannot detect an even difference between lexical entries, and an even function cannot detect an odd difference. Within bases restricted to definite-parity columns, at least two columns are required to distinguish all entries exactly when the inventory contains two pairs with nonzero differences of opposite parity. With symmetric thresholds, three utterances suffice to impose the requirement: *some* and *all* have an even difference, whereas *no* and *all* have an odd difference (nb appdx. C §5). The implemented basis supplies both directions. Separation alone could use a single column of mixed parity, but this coordinate would combine tilt and width. The two-dimensional requirement is thus conditional on the parity restriction, while the choice to separate tilt and width supplies the representational motivation. §5.2.2 considers how the two effects could be represented with one utility dimension at each of several levels.

## 3.6 Two read-outs

The settled state \(x^\ast=(\varphi_S^\ast,\varphi_u^\ast)\) supports two read-outs, whose domains must be distinguished. Under the construction's delta approximation, the posterior over state configurations is approximated by a point mass at \(x^\ast\). The **delta read-out** is accordingly the settled activity of the state units (nbtc 3 §4, item 5). In Bogacz's examples these units estimate world quantities, so their activities identify the most likely values of these quantities (Bogacz, 2017, §§2.2–3). Here the situation units carry log-weights, and the delta is centred on the most likely field configuration. A statement about a proportion on the world scale requires a further statistic of this field. The exact posterior over configurations, its delta approximation and a density over the world coordinate are therefore three distinct objects.

One available statistic is the mode position, the node at which \(\varphi_S^\ast\) is largest. Identifying the mode requires no normalization, although selection across the grid lies outside the implemented network dynamics. Empirically, every configuration under *some* covered by the audit had a single local maximum (nb appdx. A). In these configurations, neighbouring comparisons suffice to locate the mode, although such comparisons would require lateral access absent from the architecture (§6.5). The mode position criterion asks whether the selected node lies outside the cell of *all* defined by the threshold in §3.3 (nbtc 4, Part D). The criterion is stipulated over \(\zeta\), since the mode depends on the coordinate in which the density is expressed. The quadrature is uniform in \(\zeta\), and a Beta base world prior with positive shape parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) has an interior mode in this coordinate, at \(\zeta=\log(\alpha/\beta)\), corresponding to \(s=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\). The value \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) is the mean of the Beta distribution over \(s\), giving the two-outcome instance of MacKay's (1998, p. 80) result for the softmax basis (nbtc 4, Part D). Together, the quadrature measure and the interior mode supply the motivations for the coordinate choice introduced in §3.2.

The **normalized read-out** instead interprets the exponentiated field as a density over the world coordinate. On the grid, the normalized density is

\[
q(\zeta_k)=
\frac{\exp(\varphi_{S,k}^\ast)}
{\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_i\exp(\varphi_{S,i}^\ast)},
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_kq(\zeta_k)=1.
\tag{3.11}
\]

This additional stipulation supplies masses and expectations for comparison with probabilistic accounts, including RSA (nbtc 3 §4, item 5, Eq. 12).[^q] The value \(q(\zeta_k)\) is a density value; its quadrature contribution to a mass is \(w_kq(\zeta_k)\). Exponentiation could be performed at each node, but normalization requires information across the grid, and neither operation belongs to the architecture's dynamics. The normalization issue is the one discussed in §2.2, including the proposal for normalizing discrete distributions in a separate circuit.

Exponentiation and division by a positive constant preserve the location of a maximum, so the settled field and \(q\), read as a density in \(\zeta\), share their mode. Expressing the same distribution as a density in \(s\) introduces the logit map's Jacobian and can move this mode. Tempering has a different effect: halving the log-field leaves its mode unchanged but changes normalized masses. These properties motivate the paper's preference for the mode position criterion as an evaluation of the settled state. The mode avoids the added normalization and is unaffected by this tempering, although its selection remains external to the network. Both read-outs are reported: the mode position criterion leads the evaluation, while the mass shift and mass position criteria provide auxiliary comparisons with RSA and other probabilistic accounts (§4.1.2). Their agreement is not independent corroboration, and disagreement does not make either read-out a correction of the other.

## 3.7 How the function of the architecture differs from the function that RSA specifies

The architecture differs from the RSA account of §1.2 in where alternatives, world knowledge and lexical constraints enter interpretation. These differences follow from the specified objectives and read-outs before the evaluation in §4. There is no speaker in the prediction chain, and the objective for a presented utterance contains no contribution from an unobserved alternative. With the configuration and gain held fixed, changing the alternatives available on the trial therefore leaves the settled belief unchanged. Other entries influence interpretation through the gain learned across exposure (§3.4). In RSA, by contrast, the normalization of \(S_1\) over available utterances makes the pragmatic listener sensitive to alternatives within the trial.

The accounts also give different roles to world knowledge. In the RSA formulation of §1.2, the world state prior \(P(s)\) multiplies the truth indicator in \(L_0\) and the speaker likelihood in \(L_1\). Wonky-world RSA allows the listener to infer whether an elicited prior or a uniform fallback applies (§1.3.2). The present architecture keeps the base world prior fixed in \(g_L\) and infers the situation field jointly with utility. Finite lexical strength makes the entry defeasible against the supplied base world prior (§3.2). Thus, the flexibility lies in how strongly the lexical entry restricts the inferred field, while the supplied base world prior remains fixed.

The difference can be seen in the treatment of *some*. For the truth-conditional inventory ⟨*no*, *some*, *all*⟩ with equal costs, *some* is the only true utterance at intermediate states. Consequently, \(S_1(\textit{some}\mid s)=1\) at intermediate states, whereas *some* competes with *all* at the all-state. With positive speaker optimality and positive prior support for the relevant states, RSA preserves the literal listener's relative weights among intermediate states and reduces the all-state's weight relative to them. In the present architecture, stationarity of Eq. (3.7) in \(\varphi_S\) instead gives

\[
\varphi_S^\ast
=\frac12(\ell_0-\varphi_L)
+\frac12\theta_uB\varphi_u^\ast.
\tag{3.12}
\]

The displacement from the tempered literal field lies in the span of \(B\), so utility can alter tilt and width across the whole field (§3.5). The direction of change at the all-state depends on the base world prior through \(c_y\), rather than following from competition between *some* and *all* in a speaker model. The utility projection and RSA informativity do take related inputs: \(c_y\) projects the softened \(\log L_0\), while the RSA speaker uses \(\log L_0\) itself. However, because \(B^{\mathsf T}W\mathbf1_K=0\), the utility projection discards the constant component containing the literal listener's log normalizer. RSA's comparison of utterance informativity depends on this normalizer, so the two operations retain different information from \(\log L_0\) (nb appdx. D §5).

Disconnecting utility reveals a further difference between the literal-listener constructions. At the implemented variances, Eq. (3.12) reduces to \(\tfrac12(\ell_0-\varphi_L)\). Normalizing this tempered field yields a density proportional to the square root of the softened literal listener, whereas literal Bayesian conditioning in §1.2 uses the untempered product. The situation level's Gaussian term causes this tempering. Finite lexical strength produces a second difference: in the truth-conditional RSA formulation considered here, \(L_0\) and \(L_1\) exclude false states regardless of the world state prior. The architecture instead assigns them a finite penalty, leaving the normalized read-out with positive density at every represented node. A sufficiently concentrated base world prior can outweigh this penalty. This RSA formulation has no lexical-strength parameter corresponding to \(\Lambda\), and hence no corresponding override threshold (§4.1.5).

The shared literal listener is recovered under a combined limit: utility is disconnected, the situation level's own error contribution is removed through \(\sigma_S\to\infty\), and \(\Lambda\to\infty\) imposes hard exclusion (§3.2; nbtc 4, Part C). In this limit the base world prior \(p_0=e^{\ell_0}\), expressed on the common coordinate and measure, plays the role of RSA's world state prior. Increasing lexical strength alone imposes exclusion while leaving the tempering term in place. The accounts therefore share a limiting literal listener, while finite lexical strength and the situation level's Gaussian term distinguish the implemented architecture from this limit.

# 4. Evaluation

The evaluation examines two phenomena that a model of scalar resolution should accommodate. §4.1 asks whether the architecture predicts scalar implicature under *some*, with the number of atoms fixed at \(n=10\). §4.2 compares the architecture's threshold semantics with Xiang et al.'s (2022) adjective judgements, varying both \(n\) and lexical strength \(\Lambda\). Both evaluations use two utility dimensions, \(m=2\), and learn \(\theta_u^\ast\) separately for each configuration. As established in §§3.4–3.5, the shared-gain update departs from locality at this dimensionality; the results therefore evaluate the architecture with this departure in place.

## 4.1 Scalar implicature

### 4.1.0 Hypotheses

The hypothesis concerns whether the architecture predicts *some but not all* wherever the lexical entries retain their force against the base world prior. **H1** states that, throughout this domain, the settled field under *some* has its mode outside the cell of *all*. **H0** states that at least one configuration within the domain places the settled mode inside this cell. Because the architecture is deterministic, a counterexample within the domain is sufficient to refute H1. The domain restriction excludes configurations at which the base world prior overrides an entry: under the override convention of §3.2, the utterance then supplies no stable literal interpretation for an implicature to enrich. The override threshold formalizes this restriction (nbtc 6, Eq. 40). H1 is assessed by the mode position criterion defined in §4.1.2. Whether the mode also moves downwards from the literal baseline is a further question, addressed by the mode shift criterion. Such movement supplies a directional signature of strengthening, whereas a mode outside *all* may already be present under literal conditioning. The evaluation therefore reports strengthening alongside the test of H1, with the outcome stated in §4.1.7 after the baselines, criteria and results have been presented.

### 4.1.1 Set-up

To distinguish strengthening from an interpretation already supplied by literal conditioning, the comparisons use the literal listener \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) as their baseline. As defined in §§3.2 and 3.7, \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) is obtained by normalizing the exponential of the field \(\ell_0-\varphi_L\). The corresponding read-out from the fully settled field is \(q_H\) (§3.6). The literal field follows algebraically when utility is disconnected, \(\theta_u=0\), and the situation level's own Gaussian contribution is removed through \(\sigma_S\to\infty\). Thus, the literal baseline is a limiting comparison within the specified construction, whose form depends on the placement of the base log-prior \(\ell_0\) in \(g_L\). At the implemented unit variances, disconnecting utility instead yields the tempered field \(\tfrac12(\ell_0-\varphi_L)\). This tempered control corresponds to the stipulated initial gain \(\theta_u(0)=0\) in the exposure flow (§3.4).

The distinction between the literal baseline and the tempered control allows the total change in mass on the cell of *all* to be decomposed into two contributions: the change from \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) to the tempered control, and the subsequent change from the tempered control to \(q_H\). The factor one half follows from the unit variances and reflects the situation level's own Gaussian term. Measuring only from the tempered control would omit this contribution, even though tempering is part of the architecture's inference and can change the all-region mass in either direction (§4.1.4). The literal baseline therefore captures the combined effects of tempering and utility, while also providing the comparison with RSA's literal listener developed below.

A second baseline choice concerns whether to measure change from the literal listener or from the base world prior. A change from the prior includes the exclusion imposed by the lexical entry, so literal conditioning alone could produce the measured shift. The present shift criteria concern changes beyond this literal contribution. For *some*, however, the distinction is numerically small under the five priors considered here: the entry excludes only the cell of count zero, the base-prior and literal modes coincide, and their masses on the cell of *all* nearly agree (§4.1.4; nbtc 4, Part C). The baseline choice has greater consequences when an entry excludes a larger region. In §4.2, movement is assessed against the elicited prior because the hypothesis there concerns movement towards an extreme, including the contribution of literal conditioning.

In addition to separating literal conditioning from subsequent inference, \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) provides a point of comparison with RSA's \(L_0\). Calling the baseline a literal listener identifies this correspondence, subject to the distinctions established in §3.7. The base world prior \(p_0=e^{\ell_0}\), expressed on the same coordinate and measure, takes the role of RSA's world state prior. At finite \(\Lambda\), lexical exclusion supplies a softened truth set; hard truth-conditional exclusion is recovered as \(\Lambda\to\infty\). The finite penalty also makes the entry defeasible against a sufficiently concentrated base world prior, while this prior remains fixed. These properties distinguish the architecture from both the truth-conditional RSA formulation and the revision of world-prior assumptions in wonky-world RSA. The baseline nevertheless provides a principled comparison: removing utility and the situation level's own Gaussian term yields \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\), whose hard-exclusion limit agrees with \(L_0\). In the accounts compared here, the literal listener is constructed from a supplied prior and semantics; consequently, a measured departure from the literal listener remains relative to these modelling choices (§2.5).

The five-prior comparison uses \(\Lambda=512\), at which every lexical entry holds under every prior in the comparison (nbtc 4b). This strength separates the implicature question from lexical override. At \(\Lambda=8\), by contrast, override of *no* or *all* already occurs under the Gaussian and skewed priors, so interpreting their results requires distinguishing failures of lexical restriction from changes beyond the literal baseline. With lexical strength fixed, the comparison varies the base world prior. The default is \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) in \(\zeta\), inducing a logit-normal distribution over \(s\). The default is symmetric about the scale midpoint, unimodal and positive at every grid node. Three alternatives are specified directly over \(s\): \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)\), which is flat in \(s\) but not in \(\zeta\), and the mirrored pair \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,3)\) and \(\mathrm{Beta}(3,1)\), which favour the low and high ends respectively.

The fifth prior, \(\mathrm{Beta}(64,1)\), tests a more concentrated expectation of the upper endpoint. The family \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,1)\) approaches a point mass at \(s=1\) as \(\alpha\) increases, providing a representable approximation to certainty at an endpoint. A literal point mass cannot be represented by the present construction: \(s\) lies in an open interval and the log-density would be \(-\infty\) away from the point. At \(\alpha=64\), the prior's mode in \(\zeta\) is \(\log64\approx4.16\), inside the cell of *all* and below the default grid ceiling, with 95.7 per cent of the represented prior mass in the all-region. The chosen lexical strength, \(\Lambda=512\), is approximately 2.7 times the utility-disconnected estimate of the override threshold; §4.1.5 distinguishes this estimate from the threshold after gain learning. Among the five priors, only this concentrated prior places the literal mode inside *all*, and only this row changes its mode position classification after full inference (§4.1.4). The concentrated case addresses the prior-sensitivity issue raised by Degen et al. (2015), whose interpretation data were less sensitive to elicited priors than baseline RSA predicted (§1.3.1). The \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,1)\) sweep in §4.1.5 extends the comparison along this dimension.

The use of analytic prior families also determines how this evaluation relates to elicited data. H1 concerns the production of a defined implicature and can therefore be assessed without an elicited prior, although the evaluated families cover only part of the hypothesis's domain. The elicitation issues reviewed in §2.5 motivate this choice. Degen et al.'s (2015) slider priors pool one response from each participant, yielding a distribution of individual guesses rather than a direct measurement of any one listener's prior distribution. Moreover, the authors' repository contains a second elicitation for the same 90 items. This paper's comparison of these files finds similar average expected counts, 10.21 under the give-a-number slider task and 10.07 under the four-step task, with a median absolute itemwise difference of 0.91 out of 15. However, the tasks differ substantially at the all-state: the assigned probability exceeds one half for 35 items under the slider task and 16 under the four-step task, and the median all-state probabilities are 0.352 and 0.189 respectively (nb appdx. F §12). Thus, close agreement in expected counts does not secure agreement in the endpoint probability relevant to the mass criteria. Using elicited priors here would make the evaluation depend on how these responses are interpreted as model priors; §4.2 takes up this question for the adjective data.

For each of these prior configurations, the settled field is evaluated through both read-outs described in §3.6: the field mode supplies the statistic associated with the delta read-out, and \(q_H\) supplies normalized masses, with a role comparable to RSA's pragmatic listener \(L_1\). Comparing the read-outs establishes how the description of a settled state depends on the chosen statistic (§2.5). RSA and wonky-world RSA provide analytic points of comparison in this evaluation. Quantitative comparison with human judgements is undertaken in §4.2.

### 4.1.2 Evaluation criteria

The criteria locate the settled interpretation relative to the cell of *all*. On the represented grid, this cell is \([\theta_L,Z]\), the Voronoi cell of count \(n\), with lower boundary determined by \(n\) and upper boundary determined by the grid half-width \(Z\) (§3.3; nb appdx. A). Both boundaries are stipulated; the default values in this evaluation are \(n=10\) and \(Z=6\). The mode criteria concern the location of the field maximum, whereas the mass criteria integrate the normalized read-out over the cell, termed the *all-region* in reporting mass.

Write \(k^\ast=\arg\max_k\varphi_{S,k}^\ast\) for the settled mode's grid index and \(k_0=\arg\max_k\ell_{0,k}\) for the base-prior mode's index. The two mode criteria are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Mode position:}\quad &\zeta_{k^\ast}<\theta_L,\\
\text{Mode shift:}\quad &k^\ast<k_0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The mode position criterion tests H1 by asking whether the settled mode lies outside *all*. The mode shift criterion asks whether the mode has moved downwards from the base-prior mode, which coincides with the literal and tempered modes under *some* in the configurations considered here. Satisfying both criteria, termed the *mode conjunction*, combines a non-*all* interpretation with the directional signature of strengthening. Keeping the criteria distinct prevents an interpretation already present at the literal baseline from being credited to further inference.

The auxiliary mass position criterion asks whether the normalized read-out places less than half its mass in the all-region:[^qmass]

\[
q_H(\text{all}\mid\textit{some})<\frac12.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The mass shift criterion instead compares this mass with the literal baseline:

\[
\Delta_{\textit{some}}
=q_H(\text{all}\mid\textit{some})
-q_{\mathrm{lit}}(\text{all}\mid\textit{some})<0.
\tag{4.3}
\]

The *mass conjunction* requires both criteria. Literal conditioning may already place less than half the mass in the all-region, while a decrease relative to the literal baseline may still leave a majority there. The conjunction distinguishes both possibilities from a decrease that also yields a non-*all* majority. The distinction between read-outs matters here because the mass criteria operate on an exponentiated and normalized field and therefore need not preserve invariances of the field itself. Tempering, for example, preserves the location of the maximum but can alter the distribution of mass (§3.6).

The stipulated cell boundaries also affect the criteria. Under the flat prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)\) at \(\Lambda=512\), the mode position criterion and both mass criteria are satisfied at \(Z=5\) and \(Z=6\), but all three fail at the tested larger half-widths \(Z=7,8,10\) (nb appdx. A). Such failures of mode position necessarily entail failure of the mode conjunction. Whether failure also extends to the mass conjunction depends on the relation between the read-outs. Empirically,[^empirical] on the default \(K=101,Z=6\) grid, mode position and mass position agree in all 121 cells of the concentration–lexical-strength plane (nbtc 6). Consequently, every failure of mode position on this plane is also a failure of the mass conjunction. The consequence follows from the observed agreement, whereas the agreement itself is a simulation finding and need not persist on other grids.

### 4.1.3 Specification checks

The notebook checks whether the numerical implementation conforms to the specified equations before the evaluation results are interpreted. The checks compare closed-form fixed points with integrated dynamics where integration is performed, verify algebraic identities, examine the Hessian and relaxation rates, and compare local messages with finite-difference derivatives. Agreement with integrated dynamics is assessed relative to the stopping tolerance (nbtc 3 §8; nbtc 4, *Reporting statistics*). The strong-lexicon comparison at \(\Lambda=512\) is evaluated through the closed-form fixed points (nbtc 4b).

Grid checks distinguish numerical resolution from truncation. Increasing the node count \(K\) at fixed half-width stabilizes \(\mathbb E[s]\), whereas increasing \(Z\) at approximately fixed node spacing continues to change the expectation. The half-width therefore carries a substantive commitment about the represented domain (§§3.2 and 6.5). Changing \(Z\) also changes the measure under which the utility basis \(B\) is orthonormalized and the literal field is projected (§3.3). As a result, widening the grid affects the utility contribution to the settled field as well as extending the upper boundary of the all-region.

To distinguish the effects of gain growth and utility re-projection, nb appdx. A compares several fields over the hypothesis's domain. Empirically, at each tested half-width, holding \(\theta_u\) at its \(Z=6\) value gives the same count of configurations satisfying mode position as relearning the gain. The large-gain-limit field gives the same counts. Two further comparisons examine how changes to the utility field affect mode position. Extending the fixed \(Z=6\) utility field onto the wider grid largely preserves modes outside *all*, although the \(Z=8\) comparison has exceptions. Retaining the \(Z=6\) basis polynomials while recomputing the projection over the wider domain further separates the effect of the integration domain from the effect of rebuilding the basis. The resulting comparisons show a contribution from the wider projection domain at \(Z=8\) and \(Z=10\). Note that the fixed-field and fixed-basis comparisons are *algebraic counterfactual manipulations*: comparison fields are constructed by altering terms in the closed-form expression for the settled state, rather than by evaluating another admissible configuration of the specified network. Their purpose is to isolate how the utility field and its projection contribute to the change in mode position. The fixed-gain comparison, by contrast, retains the network equations and holds \(\theta_u\) at its \(Z=6\) value, and therefore constitutes a control within the network. Taken together, the comparisons support attributing the changing pattern to utility re-projection rather than to growth of the learned gain.

Whereas the half-width comparisons change the represented domain, node-count refinement examines sensitivity to its numerical resolution. For the nine prior-and-strength configurations reported in nbtc 4 and 4b, all four criterion classifications agree with the fine-grid reference down to \(K=81\), with the first differences on the descending ladder appearing at \(K=61\). The larger concentration–lexical-strength plane already contains some grid-dependent classifications, so agreement for these nine configurations does not extend to the entire sweep. Nor does the ladder establish a minimum adequate node count: changing \(K\) changes the position of nodes relative to the lexical boundary as well as field resolution, and the reported comparison does not separate these effects (nb appdx. A).

\[
\varphi_S^\ast(\theta_u)-\varphi_S^\ast(0)
=\frac{\theta_u}{2}B\varphi_u^\ast(\theta_u).
\tag{4.4}
\]

As the magnitude of the gain increases without bound, the field approaches

\[
\varphi_S^\ast(\theta_u)
\longrightarrow
\frac12\bigl(I+BB^{\mathsf T}W\bigr)(\ell_0-\varphi_L),
\qquad |\theta_u|\to\infty.
\tag{4.5}
\]

Here \(I\) is the identity on the situation grid. Equation (4.5) doubles the tempered control's component in \(\operatorname{span}B\), leaving the orthogonal component unchanged (nbtc 4, Eqs. 23–24). The mechanism is therefore amplification of the literal field's utility projection. Under \(\mathrm{Beta}(64,1)\), the width coordinate is negative; amplifying this coordinate lowers both tails of the field, including the all-region (nb appdx. C §8).

### 4.1.5 Lexical strength by the concentration of the base world prior

The five-prior comparison raises the question of how the criteria depend jointly on prior concentration and lexical strength. The \(11\times11\) sweep over \(\alpha\) and \(\Lambda\) examines this dependence within the family \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,1)\). At the default \(Z=6\), \(K=101\), the base-prior mode lies inside *all* in 66 cells. Inference moves the mode outside *all* in nine of these cells, satisfying mode shift in all nine. Across the full 121-cell plane, mode position holds in 59 cells and the mode conjunction in 13. The corresponding mass conjunction holds in 33 cells. Of the 77 cells where the literal listener places at least half its mass in the all-region, 15 fall below one half after inference, necessarily satisfying mass shift. The nine cells where inference changes mode position form a subset of these 15 mass changes (nbtc 6). These counts distinguish a criterion satisfied by the settled state from a criterion newly satisfied through inference. The counts also depend on the represented domain. Varying the half-width at approximately fixed node spacing gives the following counts over the full plane (nb appdx. A):

| Grid half-width \(Z\) | Mode position | Mode conjunction | Mass conjunction |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 77 | 37 | 68 |
| 6 | 59 | 13 | 33 |
| 7 | 26 | 0 | 0 |
| 8 | 14 | 0 | 0 |
| 10 | 12 | 0 | 0 |

At the three larger tested half-widths, neither conjunction is satisfied. The absence of conjunctions also constrains changes beyond the literal baseline: moving a mode from inside to below *all* necessarily entails a downward shift, just as reducing all-region mass from at least one half to below one half entails a negative mass shift. A position criterion newly satisfied through inference must therefore satisfy the corresponding conjunction. Since both conjunctions are absent at these half-widths, inference produces no new satisfaction of either position criterion there.

Returning to the default half-width \(Z=6\), the dependence on lexical strength can be summarized by a *floor*: the least sampled \(\Lambda\) at and above which a criterion holds throughout the sampled ladder. Where such a floor exists, mode position and mass position have the same floor at every \(\alpha\). Empirically, from \(\alpha=16\) to 128, their sampled floors also coincide with the sampled strength required to retain the lexical entry. Mode shift is never satisfied at \(\alpha\le8\), so no mode-conjunction floor exists there. Some criteria also have no floor within the sampled range at the highest concentrations (nbtc 6). The mass criteria impose opposing requirements as concentration increases. More prior mass on *all* allows a negative shift at a lower lexical strength, while reducing the remaining all-region mass below one half requires a higher strength. The mass-shift floor consequently falls as the mass-position floor rises, with the two crossing between \(\alpha=8\) and 16. Their maximum, the floor for the mass conjunction, traces a V over the concentrations where the conjunction is attainable. The mode conjunction lacks the descending branch because the modes of the diffuse priors move upwards. From \(\alpha=32\), the mode and mass conjunctions share the ascending branch set by position; at \(\alpha=16\), the mode conjunction requires \(\Lambda=256\), compared with 64 for the mass conjunction. At the highest concentration, \(\alpha=1024\), saturation also interrupts the falling mass-shift floor: only the final strength, 2048, produces a shift resolved beyond the numerical zero band (nbtc 6).

An analytic approximation relates the override threshold to prior concentration and predicate granularity. With utility disconnected,

\[
\Lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}\approx\alpha\log(2n).
\tag{4.6}
\]

Lexical strength must therefore increase approximately linearly with concentration, with the slope determined by the predicate's granularity. The notebook's utility-disconnected checks agree with the approximation to about four per cent. Empirically, relearning \(\theta_u^\ast\) raises the measured slope by 36–45 per cent relative to the tempered control in the reported threshold probes (nbtc 6, Eqs. 40–41). Equation (4.6) supplies the disconnected comparison; the learned network requires a stronger lexical penalty in these probes. The sweep manipulates \(\Lambda\), which is not elicited from participants. Its role in this evaluation follows from the architecture's override stipulation (§3.2), which makes lexical strength the parameter governing whether an entry withstands the supplied base world prior.

### 4.1.6 Where the conjunctions first hold, and integration cost

The preceding results describe the settled fields at the asymptotic gain. A further question is how far learning must proceed before the conjunctions are satisfied, since the gain also determines inference cost. Under \(\mathrm{Beta}(64,1)\) at \(\Lambda=512\), mode position and the mode conjunction first hold at \(|\theta_u|=2.024\), where \(\lambda_{\max}(H)=6.1\). The mass conjunction first holds at \(|\theta_u|=2.126\), where \(\lambda_{\max}(H)=6.5\), requiring a state-to-error timescale separation of approximately 26 under Eq. (3.9). The slow update crosses both gain thresholds on its first update, and the criteria remain satisfied at every subsequently checked point along the gain ray to \(\theta_u^\ast\) (nbtc 4b). With the implemented stopping tolerance, the slow flow halts at \(\theta_u=34.695\) after seven updates. The integrated inference dynamics at this gain satisfy both conjunctions, with a required timescale separation of 4,823. By comparison, the asymptotic gain is \(\theta_u^\ast=1407.77\). Inference at this gain is estimated to require \(3.98\times10^8\) steps with the implemented integrator and a separation of \(7.9\times10^6\) (nbtc 4, *Integration cost and conditioning*; nbtc 4b). Thus, the conjunctions are demonstrated in integrated dynamics well before the asymptotic gain, whose results are reported from the closed form. Along the checked continuation towards the asymptote, further gain growth leaves the conjunction classifications unchanged while increasing inference cost.

### 4.1.7 What the results support

At the stipulated half-width \(Z=6\), the results support H1 across the evaluated configurations where the lexical entries hold, provided the grid resolves the relevant boundary. Mode position is satisfied under all five priors at \(\Lambda=512\). ## 4.2 Scale classes and Xiang et al. (2022)

The adjective evaluation asks whether the same architecture can capture movement towards a scale's upper extreme and the differences between absolute adjective classes. Xiang et al.'s (2022) data provide complementary evidence: elicited priors, truth-value judgements and degree judgements following an utterance. Relating these measurements to the architecture requires a mapping from adjective classes to lexical entries and from the five response positions to the represented scale. The evaluation distinguishes three questions: whether literal conditioning produces movement towards the upper extreme, how closely the settled profiles fit the degree judgements, and whether the stipulated entries capture the antonym relation.

### 4.2.0 Hypotheses

**H1** states that the present architecture predicts *extreme-favouring* behaviour in complete-scale adjectives, conditional on the stipulated class-to-entry map and fitted lexical strength. Here, *extreme-favouring* means upward movement in mean scale position relative to the elicited prior, with positions ordered in the adjective's own orientation. The term describes a change in belief, whereas Xiang et al.'s (2022) *endpoint-oriented* describes the thresholds reconstructed from truth-value judgements (§1.7.3). H1 is assessed on the literal listener \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) in all four conditions, comprising the two absolute classes and two image types, at every \(n\) on the evaluation ladder. **H0** states that in at least one such condition the literal listener does not move towards the upper extreme. The outcome is reported in §4.2.3. The choice to assess H1 on the literal listener rests on the elicitation assumptions of §4.2.1. The set-up is also motivated by Conjecture I, concerning unstable atomicity and lexical strength, and Conjecture II, concerning the form of the classes' truth regions. Their role here is to motivate the joint variation of \(n\) and \(\Lambda\) and the examination of the class-to-entry map. H1 tests the direction of movement under this set-up; the conjectures are discussed separately in §§5.3.1–5.3.2.

### 4.2.1 Set-up

The data contain 96 items from Xiang et al. (2022), of which 56 belong to the two absolute classes modelled here. The remaining 40 relative-adjective items require a context-dependent threshold that the present inventory does not supply. As in §4.1, the architecture uses two utility dimensions. The absolute classes are assigned the following entries, read in each adjective's own orientation:

\[
\textit{maximum-standard}\mapsto\chi_{\textit{all}},
\qquad
\textit{minimum-standard}\mapsto\chi_{\textit{some}}.
\tag{4.7}
\]

The map is stipulated on the basis of the classes' maximal and non-zero standards (§1.7.3; nb appdx. F §1). The threshold \(\theta_L=\log(2n-1)\) places the corresponding entry boundaries near the two endpoints (§3.3). Endpoint anchoring is thus part of the architecture's scale structure. Xiang et al. infer threshold distributions from their truth-value data without making this class-to-entry assignment; the relation between the stipulated map and the classes' truth regions is examined in §5.3.2, and the comparison with a semantic convention in §6.1.

The parameter \(n\) represents the predicate's atomicity, which the account takes to be unstable for gradable predicates in the sense discussed by Chierchia (2010; §1.8). The evaluation varies \(n\) independently of the number of response options. Because the current architecture clamps the lexical field, atomicity remains fixed within each run. Varying \(n\) across configurations supplies a comparison for the future proposal to learn the threshold (§5.3.1); whether the response options themselves affect atomicity remains open. Lexical strength is varied alongside \(n\) because Conjecture I concerns their relation, while the override stipulation permits variation in \(\Lambda\) (§3.2). At \(Z=6\), the sweep uses \(n\in\{2^2,\ldots,2^7\}\) and \(\Lambda\in\{2^2,\ldots,2^{10}\}\). The next power, \(n=2^8\), places \(\theta_L\) beyond the grid boundary. A subsequent search locates fits between neighbouring points of the sweep, with one pair \((n,\Lambda)\) per class shared across image types. The located pairs are the only quantities fitted to human response data in this paper (nb appdx. F §§2–3).

This parameterization also determines how the results can be compared with Xiang et al.'s models. Their LG pragmatic listener infers the threshold, whereas their QF speaker already knows a conventional threshold distribution; both take the elicited prior as a listener prior (§1.7.3). In the present architecture, fixing \(n\) fixes one threshold. A finite \(\Lambda\) softens the exclusion penalty without distributing the boundary's location. The utility level carries statistics of exposure across trials, with no speaker reasoning represented within the current trial (§§3.4 and 5.2.1). The fitted quantities are therefore lexical resolution and strength, rather than speaker rationality or utterance cost. Given these differences in how thresholds are specified, the present \(R^2\) comparisons assess dependence on lexical parameters and utility within this architecture (nb appdx. F §2).

To connect the represented scale to Experiment 3, the five images are mapped to a partition called R1. On an absolute scale, one extreme image depicts the minimum adjective's degree zero, such as a straight nail or a plain circle. This image is position 1 in the minimum adjective's ordering and position 5 in the maximum adjective's ordering. R1 assigns the image the endpoint's Voronoi cell at the chosen \(n\), precisely the region excluded by *some* or retained by *all*. The other four images receive four equal-width cells over the remaining scale. Each elicited probability is spread uniformly in \(s\) within its cell, then transformed to the \(\zeta\) coordinate. The same cells provide the bins in which the normalized read-out is compared with Experiment 3's response proportions (nb appdx. F §2).

The partition makes the entry boundary coincide with a bin boundary at every \(n\), so within-bin allocation does not determine how much of a bin the entry retains. The opposite extreme image receives no endpoint status because the stimuli do not establish that the depicted degree exhausts the scale. Equal widths for the four remaining cells are a stipulation in the absence of stated image spacing; assigning equally spaced images to nearest-neighbour cells would instead place the far image at the other endpoint. At a common \(n\), reversing the images between a minimum adjective and its maximum antonym mirrors one physical partition. The class-specific fits may, however, select different resolutions; the shared-partition interpretation therefore applies to the common-\(n\) comparisons. Throughout the evaluation, *mean scale position* denotes the expectation of the image index, from 1 to 5 in the adjective's own ordering. One image set, `curved_greenline`, received no Experiment 3 response for either adjective. Excluding these two items from scoring leaves 155 item-by-position rows in the maximum class and 115 in the minimum class (nb appdx. F §3).

Using R1 to translate elicited priors also requires a decision about what the responses measure. Xiang et al. (2022) use the aggregate of Experiment 1 as their model prior, read Experiment 3 as a posterior judgement conditioned on the utterance, and connect Experiment 2's truth judgements to their models through linking hypotheses. The present comparison likewise takes Experiment 1's distribution as the base world prior, with \(\ell_0=\log p_0\), supplying the input required for each item's prediction. As discussed in §2.5, however, elicited responses depend on the task and response process, so their identification with the prior operative in interpretation remains a stipulation. Xiang et al.'s largely uniform slider priors from the same image sets provide a concrete reason to examine this dependence. To assess dependence on this choice, a control assigns every item equal mass at each of the five positions. At the same fitted \(n\) and \(\Lambda\), the maximum class's full-network fit is nearly unchanged, \(R^2=0.992\) under the uniform prior compared with 0.993 under the elicited prior. The minimum class gives 0.429 compared with 0.456. The notebook also reports fits located again under the uniform prior and the comparisons by image type (nb appdx. F §10). The role of the prior differs between the qualitative and numerical comparisons: reweighting the excluded lower region determines the direction under the support conditions stated in §4.2.3, whereas the predicted profile and fit depend on how prior mass is distributed.

A second stipulation links Experiment 3's pooled choices to the read-out masses in R1. Each participant supplies one choice, so this comparison assumes choices sampled in proportion to these masses. Franke et al.'s (2016) analysis of single-number responses instead favours a link to the mode of an individual's belief (§2.5). The task also leaves open whether a literal or pragmatic judgement is elicited: asking for a degree does not itself identify a contribution from utility. Both \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) and the full-network read-out are therefore reported, with the tempered control used to separate tempering from utility. The third stipulation is the decision to judge H1 on \(q_{\mathrm{lit}}\) against the elicited prior.

These choices give the fit statistics a different role from Xiang et al.'s (2022) comparative-fit analysis. They evaluate shared pragmatic parameters across classes, initially at rationality \(\lambda=3\) and cost 2 and subsequently across 110 settings, comparing the pragmatic listener with Experiment 3. Here, a lexical pair is fitted per class, and \(R^2\) describes the fit of both read-outs and its location in \((n,\Lambda)\); the direction of movement provides the test of H1. Experiment 2 serves a further purpose. Whereas Xiang et al. use the truth judgements to reconstruct threshold distributions, the present evaluation uses these judgements to assess the antonym relation (§4.2.6), treating each mean judgement as the truth value of the image's cell.

Learning uses the exposure ensemble \(\{\chi,\ker\chi\}\) stipulated in §3.4. The kernel of \(\chi_{\textit{some}}\) is \(\chi_{\textit{no}}\), while the kernel of \(\chi_{\textit{all}}\) has no named utterance in the inventory. Note that the ensemble represents available lexical alternatives across exposure, whereas Xiang et al.'s design presents each participant with only one adjective of an image set. Learning from the stipulated ensemble therefore differs from learning from a participant's experimental trials. For the mixed maximum–minimum pairs, reflection onto a common physical scale at a shared threshold makes the antonym coincide with the kernel. This relation follows from the stipulated common endpoint structure; full–empty, whose members are both maximum-standard, have the separate contrary relation examined in §4.2.6.

### 4.2.2 Where the fit lies

Fit is measured by squared Pearson correlation over item-by-position rows. With \(p_i\) denoting the observed response proportion and \(\hat p_i\) the predicted mass in row \(i\),

\[
R^2=
\frac{\left[\sum_i(\hat p_i-\overline{\hat p})(p_i-\bar p)\right]^2}
{\sum_i(\hat p_i-\overline{\hat p})^2\sum_i(p_i-\bar p)^2}.
\tag{4.8}
\]

The bars denote means over the scored rows. After evaluating the parameter ladder, the search examines integer \(n\) and intermediate \(\Lambda\) values around the best grid point. A score band collects evaluated points within 0.005 of the located maximum. A coordinate may lie in the interior, at a ladder endpoint, in a flat region, or in a band reaching the search boundary. Only an interior coordinate is bounded on both sides within this search (nb appdx. F §3).

The data are therefore consistent with the hard-entry limit and supply a lower bound on lexical strength without an upper bound within the evaluation. In the minimum class, by contrast, the full-network fit lies at the smallest sampled \(n\), with an interior \(\Lambda\) band from 21.72 to 26.91. ### 4.2.3 Matches

The observed degree profiles are broadly consistent with the stipulated class-to-entry map. In the maximum class, the top image receives 0.960 of responses for shapes and 0.929 for artefacts. In the minimum class, responses spread across the four non-zero images, with only 0.019 and 0.015 at the bottom image, corresponding to the region excluded by *some* (nb appdx. F §4). The contrast between endpoint concentration and exclusion of the bottom cell thus supports the different entries assigned to the two classes.

If the prior assigns mass to both excluded and retained regions, then this reweighting raises mean position. The simulations supply the displacement magnitudes. ### 4.2.4 Mismatches

Although the direction of movement agrees with H1, the minimum class shows substantial mismatches in profile and magnitude. Its separately fitted scores, 0.456 for the full network and 0.119 for the literal listener, remain well below the maximum class's 0.993. At the full-network fit, the minimum-class score separates into 0.094 for shapes and 0.879 for artefacts. The observed profile peaks at position 3 for shapes and position 5 for artefacts, whereas the literal listener peaks at position 3 in both conditions and the full network at position 5 in both. Each read-out therefore matches the peak of one condition but misses the other (nb appdx. F §5).

The image-type comparison reveals a related difference in magnitude and direction. The full network's shape-minus-artefact difference of −0.20 is about one quarter of the observed −0.83. The literal listener instead predicts +0.08, reversing the sign, while the elicited priors differ by −1.12. The class gaps also depend on image type in ways that the read-outs reproduce only partly:

### 4.2.5 Reach of the utility level

To interpret utility's differing effects across conditions, consider first the learned gain. The exposure ensemble makes the gain's sign depend on the represented prior through a simple cancellation. Since \(\chi+\ker\chi=\mathbf1\) and \(B^{\mathsf T}W\mathbf1=0\),

\[
\sum_y c_y
=B^{\mathsf T}W(2\ell_0-\Lambda\mathbf1)
=2B^{\mathsf T}W\ell_0.
\tag{4.9}
\]

Under the nonzero-coupling condition of §3.3, the sign of \(\theta_u^\ast\) is the sign of \(\langle\mu_u,\sum_yc_y\rangle\). For a fixed represented prior and utility basis, lexical strength and entry identity therefore do not determine this sign. The notebook verifies the cancellation across six \(n\), four \(\Lambda\) and all 54 scored items. Note that this invariance applies at a fixed prior representation: changing \(n\) changes R1 and hence the represented prior entering Eq. (4.9) (nb appdx. F §6).

At the located fits, every minimum item's gain is negative, every maximum item under shapes has a positive gain, and maximum items under artefacts divide into seven negative and nine positive gains. The notebook reports medians and sign counts because gain magnitudes diverge near the degenerate coupling described in nb appdx. B. However, the large-gain limit in Eq. (4.5) is independent of the gain's sign, and each reported settled field lies close to its corresponding limit. Substituting the limit changes no condition's \(R^2\) at three decimal places (nb appdx. F §6).

The contribution of utility is more directly assessed by movement from the tempered control. In the minimum class, utility moves mean position upwards by +0.58 for shapes and +0.83 for artefacts, whereas the data lie −0.08 and +0.80 from this control. Tempering itself moves the literal listener upwards by +0.23 and +0.25. The utility contribution consequently brings the artefact mean closer to the data while increasing the excess upward movement for shapes. In the maximum class, the two steps nearly cancel: tempering contributes −0.06 and −0.22, and utility contributes +0.06 and +0.20 (nb appdx. F §6). The projections also reveal how the two lexical entries relate to the utility basis. At every \(n\) on the ladder, the two entry indicators have identical odd, tilt-sensitive projections and opposite even, width-sensitive projections (nb appdx. F §7). The parity relation follows from the endpoint structure, linking the class mapping to the tilt and width directions of the utility basis.

### 4.2.6 The antonym relation and the flip

The class-to-entry map makes a precise prediction for a maximum adjective and its minimum antonym. At a common \(n\), \(\chi_{\textit{some}}\) is the reflection under \(s\mapsto1-s\) of \(\ker\chi_{\textit{all}}\). On one physical scale, the minimum adjective's retained region therefore coincides with the maximum adjective's excluded region. Every degree belongs to exactly one region: the entries are contradictories in the terminology of §1.7.2. At the five R1 positions, their truth indicators sum to one. The truth-indicator comparison uses the regions retained by each entry; finite lexical strength controls the read-out penalty on the excluded region. For *full* and *empty*, both maximum-standard, the prediction is instead a contrary relation, like *all* and *no*: the truth indicators sum to one at the endpoints and zero between them (nb appdx. F §8).

However, Experiment 2 departs from complementarity when mean truth judgements are read as the truth values of the image cells. The maximum–minimum sums are 1.01 at the shared endpoint but fall below one at the other positions. In the maximum adjective's ordering, positions 1–4 sum to 0.93, 0.87, 0.84 and 0.80 for shapes, and 0.96, 0.76, 0.63 and 0.56 for artefacts. The shortfall reflects declining acceptance of the minimum adjective near the shared endpoint: at artefact position 4, the maximum adjective is accepted with proportion 0.10 and the minimum adjective with 0.46. Because participants did not judge both antonyms of an image set, these are aggregate gaps relative to complementarity. The full–empty sums are within 0.05 of the contrary prediction except at position 4, where the sums are 0.18 for shapes and 0.34 for artefacts (nb appdx. F §8).

To assess the contribution of variation between participants, Experiment 2 is also analysed as individual response patterns. Each participant judged all five images of an item. A pattern that accepts every image from position \(k\) upwards is represented by a *cut* at \(k\), with \(k=6\) denoting rejection of all five images. Between 91 and 100 per cent of patterns, depending on class and image type, have this interval form. A test of unimodality of the cut distributions rejects unimodality for ten of the 24 minimum items and none of the 72 other items. The minimum-class cuts cluster near the first non-zero image and at the top image (nb appdx. F §11). Much of the top-cut concentration comes from 23 participants: nine of 56 in the shape condition and 14 of 57 in the artefact condition mark only the top image on at least half their interval-shaped minimum-item responses. They also choose a top cut for 95–100 per cent of their interval-shaped relative-item responses. Without these participants, the test rejects unimodality for no item. The maximum–minimum sums at positions 1–4 then become 0.98, 0.97, 0.95 and 0.93 for shapes, and 0.99, 0.97, 0.87 and 0.77 for artefacts. Their responses thus account for most of the aggregate shape gap, while a larger artefact gap remains. How to interpret the response pattern is left open for §5.3.2.

The remaining gap can also be examined by comparing the locations and concentration of the cut distributions. Complementarity on the five images maps a maximum cut at \(k\) to a minimum cut at \(7-k\). After excluding the 23 participants, the mean total-variation distance between the observed minimum cuts and the mapped maximum cuts is 0.06 for shapes and 0.35 for artefacts, compared with 0.15 and 0.48 with all participants. The mean share at the modal cut is 0.94 for minimum adjectives and 1.00 for maximum adjectives under shapes, and 0.79 and 0.86 under artefacts. The modal cuts differ in none of 11 shape sets and three of 12 artefact sets (nb appdx. F §11). Thus, after the exclusion, shapes show close agreement in both cut locations and distributions, while a larger discrepancy remains for artefacts.

The antonym relation also defines the *flip* relevant to interpreting the class asymmetry. Every minimum adjective in the stimulus set has a maximum antonym on the same images: *bent* and *curved* pair with *straight*, *open* with *closed*, *striped* and *spotted* with *plain*, and *bumpy* with *smooth*. Reversing the order puts the minimum adjective's zero at the maximum adjective's upper endpoint. The elicited priors mirror one another exactly to the reported precision, with a largest difference of 0.000. Under the stipulated mapping, the minimum truth region is obtained by complementing the maximum region and reversing orientation. This combined operation is the flip. Full–empty reverses orientation while retaining maximum-standard status, providing a comparison in which orientation changes without a change between minimum and maximum standards (§5.1.1; nb appdx. F §9).

To examine whether the minimum-class discrepancy remains when the stipulated entry is replaced by measured truth judgements, an *empirical literal listener* is constructed for each item. Let \(\pi_k\) be the elicited prior probability of position \(k\) and \(t_k\) its mean Experiment 2 truth judgement. The construction is

\[
L_0^{\mathrm{emp}}(k)
=\frac{\pi_k t_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{5}\pi_j t_j}.
\tag{4.10}
\]

Equation (4.10) treats the mean truth judgement as a soft truth function and uses the elicited prior to construct a population-level reference, pooling potentially different thresholds across participants. The comparison covers three to eight items per adjective, with separate participant samples in Experiments 2 and 3, and therefore assesses agreement between aggregate responses across tasks. Against Experiment 3, the resulting \(R^2\) is 0.995 for maximum-standard shapes and 0.960 for maximum-standard artefacts, but 0.196 and 0.261 for the corresponding minimum conditions. By adjective, maximum-class fits range from 0.92 to 1.00, including *full* at 0.987 and *empty* at 0.997; minimum-class fits range from 0.00 to 0.57 (nb appdx. F §9).

The direction of the remaining difference is consistent across adjectives. Experiment 3's mean position lies above the empirical literal listener for every minimum adjective, by +0.30 for *spotted* to +0.70 for *bent*, and slightly below the reference for every maximum adjective, by 0.03–0.13. Pooling by image type gives minimum-class upward departures of +0.30 for shapes and +0.78 for artefacts. Let \(\mathrm{TVJ}_{\max}\) denote the maximum antonym's mean truth judgement. Constructing the minimum truth function through the antonym instead, as \(1-\mathrm{TVJ}_{\max}(6-k)\) at position \(k\), gives \(R^2=0.244\) for shapes and 0.062 for artefacts, compared with 0.196 and 0.261 using the minimum adjective's own judgements. The reverse construction is reported in nb appdx. F §9. Neither version accounts closely for the minimum class's degree judgements. The upward minimum-class departure also persists when the 23 participants are excluded from the truth-judgement analysis: the observed mean remains +0.28 above the empirical literal listener for shapes and +0.88 for artefacts (nb appdx. F §11). Thus, their response pattern accounts for much of the antonym gap, while the discrepancy between truth-based literal conditioning and the elicited degree judgement remains.

Taken together, the comparisons associate the upward departure with the minimum class in this stimulus set. Full and empty reverse orientation but remain close to their empirical literal listeners, showing that reversal alone need not produce the same pattern. However, every minimum item also has a maximum antonym, so the design leaves open whether the departure arises from minimum-standard status or from the combined operation of reversal and complementation. This distinction motivates the analysis of the flip in §5.1. For the present architecture, the findings distinguish a limitation of the stipulated entries from the direction of the inferential adjustment. The architecture implements the flip exactly at a common threshold and therefore predicts complementary truth regions where the aggregate judgements show a gap. Nevertheless, tempering and utility move the minimum read-out upwards, in the direction of the observed departure from the empirical literal listener (§§4.2.4–4.2.5). Since the empirical and architectural literal listeners use different truth functions, their departures are compared here in direction. §5.1 considers whether a mechanism acting at the flip could account for the remaining asymmetry.

# 5. Conjectures regarding an additional level

## 5.0 The conjectures

The preceding analysis leaves two connected questions: how the architecture might represent the observed relation between antonyms, and how an additional level could resolve alternatives under the construction's constraints. This section develops this proposal through four conjectures. Conjecture I relates unstable atomicity to weaker lexical strength (§5.3.1). Conjecture II proposes that, in an adjective's own ordering, its truth region extends from a cut to the top of the scale, with the classes differing in the cut's location (§5.3.2). Conjecture III proposes that a minimum adjective and its maximum antonym share a scale without partitioning it (§5.3.3). Conjecture IV links lexicalization to the availability of a settled literal reading: a quantifier-type entry requires such a reading, whereas an adjective does not, and the O corner, *not all*, lacks the required reading (§5.3.4).

These claims concern different parts of the proposal: Conjecture I concerns the existing lexical level, whereas Conjecture III revises the complementary-region relation assigned to a maximum adjective and its minimum antonym at a common threshold. Conjectures II and IV concern an additional *alternatives level*, specified in §5.2 and awaiting implementation. Although the evaluations in §4 were not designed to test the conjectures, Experiment 2's individual responses subsequently provided a limited check of one prediction of Conjecture II (§§4.2.6 and 5.3.2). The argument first locates the asymmetry between antonyms, then develops the alternatives level before returning to the premises and derivational requirements of each conjecture. The term *conjecture* is reserved for these four claims; other underived elements of §5.2 remain parts of the proposed design.

## 5.1 The problem of asymmetry between zero and one

The adjective comparison raises a question about which differences between antonyms require an extension of the architecture. Differences in the extent of their truth regions can arise within the existing lexical relation, whereas a failure of complementarity calls this relation into question. The argument therefore begins by specifying the sense in which antonyms share a scale and the transformation relating their entries. The cut comparison then motivates two possible explanations for departures from the lexical relation: an asymmetry in affirmability and independently determined cuts.

### 5.1.1 Zero, one, and a scale's identity

Zero and one denote the bottom and top endpoints in an adjective's own ordering. Antonyms use the same dimension and degrees but impose inverse orderings (Kennedy & McNally, 2005, p. 351). Thus, the zero of one adjective is the one of its antonym. For a minimum adjective and its maximum antonym, the minimum adjective's zero and the maximum adjective's one name the same degree. Under the endpoint standards considered here, the minimum adjective is true above its zero and the maximum adjective at its one. Comparing the two classes therefore compares different relations to this shared endpoint. By contrast, *full* and *empty* are both maximum-standard: each is true at the upper endpoint of its own ordering. Their reversal supplies the orientation comparison in §4.2.6, while the question of whether a mixed maximum–minimum pair partitions the scale motivates Conjecture III (§5.3.3).

The phrase *same scale* requires a criterion of identity. Although a scale should ultimately be defined by a single criterion, the choice remains open here. Each use of *same* therefore specifies the structure being identified. At the least restrictive level, a scale is a set of degrees, and bijections identify sets of the same size. If order is retained without privileging a direction, the admissible maps are increasing or decreasing bijections; for a real interval with its usual topology, these are also the homeomorphisms. Preserving a directed order restricts the maps to increasing bijections, whereas preserving a metric restricts them to isometries, which preserve distance. A property is invariant under a given criterion of identity when the corresponding maps preserve the property.

Each criterion supports a different part of the argument. Truth regions, their kernels and whether two regions partition the scale can be specified on a set of degrees. Antonyms share this set irrespective of whether both adjectives appear in an exposure ensemble. Openness and closedness, and the location of cuts in an order, require the interval's order topology but no preferred direction. Naming one endpoint *zero*, however, requires a direction: admitting reversal makes the two endpoints interchangeable. Finally, the particular reflection \(R\colon s\mapsto1-s\), and hence a cut's metric mirror image, uses the metric supplied by the architecture's grid. Whether antonyms have mirrored cuts therefore depends on structure beyond their shared set of degrees (§§5.1.2–5.1.3).

### 5.1.2 Two reversals and the flip

Truth values and scalar degrees have distinct reversals. On the truth-value set \(\{0,1\}\), negation exchanges false and true. This set forms the two-element Boolean algebra. Its dual exchanges conjunction with disjunction and zero with one; negation is a dual isomorphism, carrying the original operations to their duals (Givant & Halmos, 2009, pp. 20–22, 100). Negation is the only bijection with this property on the two-element algebra, since the only other bijection is the identity. Here zero and one are truth values, whereas the same numerals on the degree interval \([0,1]\) label endpoints. The degree interval, in contrast, is an ordered chain rather than a Boolean algebra. With minimum and maximum as its lattice operations, only its endpoints have complements. Many maps reverse the interval's order, but the reflection \(R(s)=1-s\) also preserves distance and midpoints. In the usual topology of the interval, reflection is a homeomorphism, meaning a bijection continuous in both directions. Negation is likewise a homeomorphism when every subset of the truth-value set is declared open. The distinction between the reversals concerns their domains: one exchanges Boolean truth values, and the other reverses the metric scale on which these values are assigned.

A lexical entry connects the two domains by assigning a truth value to each degree. Let \(t_\chi\) denote the truth indicator of the lexical region. On the grid, \(t_\chi=1-\chi\), since the architecture's \(\chi\) marks exclusion (§3.2). For \(t_\chi\colon[0,1]\to\{0,1\}\), define its *flip*, denoted \(\mathcal Tt_\chi\), by

\[
\mathcal Tt_\chi=\neg\circ t_\chi\circ R,
\qquad
(\mathcal Tt_\chi)(s)=1-t_\chi(1-s).
\tag{5.1}
\]

The operation first reverses the degree scale and then complements the truth value assigned by the entry. The same reversal-and-complement operation applies to its exclusion indicator. At a common \(n\), the present architecture's minimum entry is the flip of its maximum antonym's entry (§4.2.6). This lexical relation provides the starting point for distinguishing differences generated by the flip from differences that require additional structure.

### 5.1.3 Locating the asymmetry by elimination

Several features of the present architecture support the lexical flip relation. Reflection preserves the grid and its measure, and the two utility-basis columns have definite parity (nb appdx. C). A single \(\theta_L\) fixes both lexical boundaries. At a common \(n\), the elicited prior of a minimum item is also the reflected prior of its maximum antonym (§4.2.6). A minimum item can therefore be represented using the reflected prior and the reflected kernel of the maximum entry. Once both entries are expressed on one physical scale, their truth regions are complements. The partition follows from the common threshold and the kernel relation stipulated by this architecture; Conjecture III proposes to relax this relation (§5.3.3).

The lexical symmetry also bears on the O corner, since the architecture can represent both endpoint cells and their kernels. In exclusion notation, *some* excludes \(\{0\}\), while *not all* excludes \(\{1\}\); the latter is the kernel of the exclusion region of *all* (§3.4). The architecture represents these endpoint exclusions through the corresponding finite-resolution cells. The O corner is therefore representable even though the inventory supplies no single utterance naming this entry. The question for Conjecture IV is why this representable entry lacks lexicalization (§§1.7.2 and 5.3.4).

For the adjective comparison, the established symmetry concerns lexical regions and their cuts. Relating this symmetry to fitted responses requires accounting for two features of the evaluation: the terminating prior \(\mu_u\) is held fixed rather than reflected, and \((n,\Lambda)\) is fitted separately for each class. The large-gain limit removes dependence on \(\mu_u\), and replacing the settled fields by this limit preserves the reported scores to three decimal places (§4.2.5). The approximation thus helps interpret the fitted responses; the cut comparison below rests on the exact complementary-region relation at a common lexical threshold.

The extent of the truth regions illustrates why complementary entries can yield different response profiles. If a region occupies a fraction \(r\) of the uniformly measured scale, its flip occupies \(1-r\). A narrow endpoint region consequently becomes a broad region above the opposite endpoint. In the hard-entry limit, conditioning on the maximum entry restricts the five-bin read-out to the endpoint bin, so subsequent redistribution within this bin cannot change mean image position. Conditioning on the minimum entry instead leaves several bins available for redistribution during settlement. At finite lexical strength, however, exclusion is softened and both comparisons admit further movement (§4.2.1). Empirically, §4.2 finds endpoint concentration for the maximum class, a broader minimum profile, and greater upward adjustment of the minimum read-out. The architecture produces this directional contrast while retaining exact complementary entries (§§4.2.4–4.2.6). Region size explains the different possibilities for movement; the direction and magnitude of the adjustment are established by the evaluation. The cut comparison addresses a different question: whether the lexical regions themselves are complementary. If the two entries are complementary on the same images, a maximum cut at \(k\) corresponds to a minimum cut at \(7-k\) (§4.2.6).

The individual-response comparison reveals two related departures from this expectation. The first concerns the concentration of cuts at the two ends: minimum cuts are less concentrated near their endpoint than maximum cuts are near theirs, even after excluding the 23 participants identified in §4.2.6. The second concerns the relation between antonyms on the shared scale. Under artefacts, the minimum cuts differ from the reflected maximum cuts, including displaced modal cuts in some image sets, whereas under shapes the distributions nearly coincide. Under matched conditions and response assumptions, an exact kernel relation would require complementary cuts independently of image type. The observed distributions therefore motivate relaxing this relation in an account of the judgements. These data distinguish the two departures only partly, because every minimum adjective has a maximum antonym on the same images: the spread and displacement of cuts are features of the same responses.

### 5.1.4 First candidate: truth and falsity are not interchangeable

The first candidate locates a possible source of asymmetry in the evidence required to affirm an assertion. In Boolean logic, a statement that is not false is true, including cases of vacuous truth. Affirmation, however, requires evidence that establishes the assertion; failure to refute the assertion is insufficient. Supervaluation (Chierchia, 2010) and Vickers's (1989) account of finite observations develop related distinctions on different grounds (§1.8). The discussion here uses Vickers's terminology while leaving the choice between these accounts open. Affirmability is also proposed as the basis for selecting between two interpretations of *some* at the alternatives level (§5.2.3).

Vickers (1989, pp. 6–8) distinguishes an affirmative assertion, true precisely where finite observations can affirm the assertion, from a refutative assertion, false precisely where such observations can refute the assertion. Their truth regions are open and closed, respectively. Negation exchanges the two kinds while retaining classical truth conditions. The proposed application to adjectives begins with an ideal continuous scale. A maximum adjective true only at its endpoint has the singleton truth region \(\{1\}\), which is closed and has empty interior in \([0,1]\). Like Vickers's assertion that a height is exactly six feet, endpoint equality can be refuted when false but cannot be affirmed by finite measurement. A minimum adjective true above its zero instead has the open truth region \((0,1]\). The distinction is between exact endpoint equality and exceeding an endpoint.

The topological formulation makes the additional structure explicit. Let \(\tau_{\mathrm{aff}}\) denote the collection of open sets on the truth-value set \(\{0,1\}\), with 1 denoting true. The affirmative topology is

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{aff}}
=\big\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{0,1\}\big\}.
\tag{5.2}
\]

The singleton \(\{1\}\) is open, whereas \(\{0\}\) is not. Negation is therefore not continuous in this topology: the inverse image of the open set \(\{1\}\) is \(\{0\}\). Note that negation remains a dual isomorphism of the Boolean algebra. The additional continuity requirement supplies the proposed asymmetry between affirmation of truth and affirmation of falsity while retaining the Boolean truth conditions. To apply the requirement to an adjective, suppose that affirmative access to a truth region consists of its interior. The interiors of a region and its complement together omit their shared boundary. Consequently, two affirmative readings can leave boundary degrees that neither reading affirms, even when the underlying Boolean regions are complementary. The omitted boundary has no width: the interiors of \(\{1\}\) and \([0,1)\) omit a single degree. Affirmative access therefore locates a failure of partition at a boundary and does not supply the gaps of positive width in §4.2.6. Reading the regions at a finite resolution does not supply these gaps either. If a degree is affirmed only when a whole neighbourhood of the degree lies in the region, the maximum adjective has no affirmable degree at any resolution, whereas its mean truth judgement at the endpoint is 1.00 (nb appdx. F §8). If the maximum adjective is instead read refutatively and the minimum adjective affirmatively, the two regions at this resolution partition the scale exactly. A gap of positive width therefore requires the separate cuts of §5.1.5.

The present architecture's finite grid, however, has a different topological structure from the continuous scale considered above. With the usual subspace topology on the grid, every subset is both open and closed, so complementation preserves openness. The architecture combines this discrete representation with a common lexical threshold and an exact kernel relation (§4.2.6). Implementing the first candidate would therefore require a representation of affirmative and refutative access beyond the grid and its lexical partition.

### 5.1.5 Second candidate, and how the two relate

The second candidate introduces independent placement of the two cuts. Reflection sends a cut at \(t\) to a cut at \(1-t\). With one \(\theta_L\), the maximum cut and the cut of the minimum antonym are reflections of one another, so their locations coincide when expressed on a shared physical scale. If the cuts are determined separately, their positions can diverge. A gap arises if the two truth regions end on opposite sides of an intervening interval; every degree in this interval then satisfies neither adjective. Separate placement thus permits a gap while retaining Boolean truth values. To account for the observed gaps, a mechanism for placing the cuts would need to determine their order and separation.

The candidates address different aspects of the observed asymmetry. The first concerns behaviour near the two ends of an adjective's own ordering. Finite observation does not itself separate these ends: Vickers's argument applies to any exact value, so exact zero and exact one are equally beyond affirmation (§5.1.4), and the reflection \(R\) is a homeomorphism (§5.1.2). This paper therefore adds a premise in its own use of affirmability: the zero of an adjective's own ordering cannot be affirmed, so a cut would approach zero without resting on zero, whereas a cut could rest at one (§5.3.2). The premise is not derived from Vickers's account, and its consequences for the cut's dynamics remain to be specified. The second candidate allows the cuts of two antonyms to depend separately on the item's quantities, providing a way for their displacement to vary with image type. The proposed failure of partition thus occurs at a boundary of no width in the first account and between independently placed cuts in the second, and only the second supplies a gap of positive width.

The candidates can also be combined: separately determined truth regions, with the minimum adjective's region open at its cut, may leave an interval of positive width between them. Whether the proposed boundary behaviour and the separate-cut construction are distinct mechanisms or two descriptions of one mechanism remains open (§5.3.2). Predicting the observed gap widths and identifying the image sets with displaced modal cuts would require a mechanism for placing the cuts. The empirical comparison also leaves minimum-standard status confounded with the flip, because every minimum adjective in the data has a maximum antonym on the same images. By contrast, *full* and *empty* reverse orientation while retaining maximum-standard status, and their responses remain close to the contrary relation predicted by the architecture (§4.2.6). Both candidates thus leave a question about the process that determines a cut. The alternatives level developed in §5.2 provides a proposed representation for this process. Conjecture II then asks whether the behaviour of its cut can connect independent placement with affirmative access (§5.3.2).

## 5.2 The proposal: an additional level representing alternatives

The proposed alternatives level would make the resolution of an opposition part of inference within a trial. The design replaces the flat inventory with a cascade of binary oppositions, each represented by a state that can change during inference. This organization has two aims: to provide a route to local gain plasticity and to represent the alternative cut whose behaviour could account for the patterns discussed in §5.1.

### 5.2.1 The present gain

The learned gain \(\theta_u^\ast\) depends on the exposure ensemble, is shared across trials and is consulted in the same way for each current utterance. Within a trial, the objective contains no unobserved utterance (§3.7). Scalar implicature produced through this route is therefore conventionalized across exposure: inference uses a learned weight whose value reflects the inventory, without selecting a current alternative. In the distinction drawn by Ronai and Xiang (2024), exposure could make the gain a vehicle for alternative accessibility, whereas the architecture provides no corresponding mechanism for selecting an alternative by its contextual relevance (§1.6). The base world prior can vary with context, but such variation does not introduce an alternative into the trial's objective. Levinson's (2000) default generalized conversational implicatures provide a precedent for inferences that need not be recomputed on each occasion. However, these defaults remain associated with an alternative set at the utterance and can be defeated within the trial (§1.6). The present gain supplies an exposure-dependent influence on interpretation; the proposed alternatives level would add a state through which the relevant opposition could be resolved for the current utterance.

### 5.2.2 Design: one opposition per level

In the cascade, each resolved opposition consists of one entry and its kernel, defined on the domain available at its level (§3.4). A fixed complementary pair can be separated by one utility direction, so a level containing this pair can use \(m=1\) (nb appdx. C §2). The proposed cascade would use this binary organization to retain one utility dimension while distributing the oppositions across levels. How one utility column serves the changing domains of the cascade remains part of the design to be derived (§5.4, item 6). A binary opposition also permits a scalar log-odds representation. Let \(\varphi_a\) denote the log-odds state of an alternatives unit, distinct from the degree coordinate \(\zeta\). In ordinary log odds, exchanging the two members reverses the sign of \(\varphi_a\). With a shifted coordinate origin, the corresponding affine involution has the form

\[
\iota_c(\varphi_a)=c-\varphi_a,
\qquad
\iota_c\bigl(\iota_c(\varphi_a)\bigr)=\varphi_a,
\tag{5.3}
\]

where \(c/2\) is the point fixed by the involution. The reference point appropriate to a restricted domain remains to be specified (§5.4, item 1). Representing the opposition directly in log odds would remove the need for a softmax across its alternatives. Affine state maps would also preserve the concavity argument of §3.4 if every new direction had a finite-variance anchor; an unanchored direction would remain flat. The concavity result would apply to inference at fixed parameters. However, allowing the alternative cut \(\theta_A\) to move introduces a further question: attraction towards the lexical cuts could make \(\mathcal F\) non-concave in \(\theta_A\) (§5.3.2). The mass position criterion illustrates the potential advantage of representing an aggregate opposition directly. The settled read-out assigns mass \(q_H(\textit{all}\mid\textit{some})\) to the region of *all*. When this mass lies strictly between zero and one, the criterion can be expressed through its log odds against the complementary region:

\[
q_H(\textit{all}\mid\textit{some})<\frac12
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\log\frac{q_H(\textit{all}\mid\textit{some})}
{1-q_H(\textit{all}\mid\textit{some})}<0.
\tag{5.4}
\]

If the new unit directly represented these aggregate odds, its sign could report mass position. Obtaining these odds from the present field would still require sums within the two regions, so the computational saving depends on the proposed representation. Mass shift remains a comparison between the settled and literal normalized beliefs and has no corresponding one-unit criterion in the proposed design (§4.1.2). The cascade would also change how tilt and width are represented. At a single level containing three utterances, one utility column either loses a direction or fixes the ratio in which tilt and width contribute (§3.5; nb appdx. C §§5–6, 9). The proposal is that these effects could instead be realized by patterns across the log-odds units associated with separate cuts. Shifts at \(-\theta_L\) and \(+\theta_L\) towards the same end would produce tilt, whereas outward or inward shifts at both cuts would change width. Because the log-odds activities would be inferred states, width could then be settled within the trial. Deriving this composition of effects requires specifying the maps between levels (§5.4).

### 5.2.3 The alternative as a cut

A resolved opposition at an alternatives level contains two members: a meaning \(u'\) of the utterance \(u\), and its negation \(\neg u'\), which supplies the alternative. Several resolutions of \(\neg u'\) may be available, but each resolution supplies one alternative to the meaning under consideration. A compound expression illustrates why the resolution need not be unique. For *Mary came*, the negation can fall on the predicate, on the subject or on both, and each choice fixes a meaning and a domain. *Mary did not come* is the complement of *Mary came* without further restriction, whereas *Mary left* is its complement only where coming and leaving exhaust the options. Given that Mary came, *someone else came* is the complement of *only Mary came*, and *someone else did not come* is the complement of *everyone else came*. These examples lie outside scalar resolution.

Scalar resolution restricts the problem to one axis, the scale. The lexical entry of *some* excludes \(\zeta\le-\theta_L\) (§3.2), and the cell of *all* is \([\theta_L,Z]\) (§4.1.2). For *some*, the alternative cut \(\theta_A\) is the inclusive upper endpoint of the resolved region: the region contains the degree at which the cut sits. On a continuous scale *all* is true at the endpoint alone, so an inclusive cut at this endpoint admits *all*. At a finite \(n\), the cell of *all* represents this endpoint and has positive width. A cut at \(+\theta_L\) is therefore stipulated to retain the whole cell, and Eq. (5.5) writes this retention as an inclusive bound at \(+\theta_L\). The two proposed readings are

\[
\begin{aligned}
(-\theta_L,\theta_A],\quad -\theta_L<\theta_A<\theta_L
&\qquad \text{with all excluded},\\
(-\theta_L,\theta_L],\quad \theta_A=\theta_L
&\qquad \text{with all admitted}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.5}
\]

In the first reading, the alternative is the region above the cut, which contains the cell of *all*; this alternative is named *all* when the cut lies immediately below the cell. In the second reading, the opposition between *all* and *not all* is left unresolved: the cut excludes no part of the domain, and neither member of the opposition is affirmed. Both are intended as interpretations of *some*. Admitting *all* leaves the all-region available without affirming *all*, which is the proposed reading of ignorance.[^ignorance] The endpoint convention makes the transition between these readings discontinuous: a cut just below \(+\theta_L\) excludes the whole cell of *all*, whereas a cut at \(+\theta_L\) retains the whole cell.

At the lower boundary, the proposal includes a further resolution in which the negation takes in both *all* and *not all*. The corresponding position of the cut is \(-\theta_L\). The interval \((-\theta_L,-\theta_L]\) is empty because the lexical entry excludes the boundary degree, so the first line of Eq. (5.5) extends to this position without a further convention. At high lexical strength, the empty region preserves the lexical exclusion below the lower cut. Conjecture II proposes that the cut approaches \(-\theta_L\) without resting there (§5.3.2), so the empty region is a limit of the resolved region and not a settled reading. Note that emptiness is a property of the truth region: the normalized read-out (3.11) still sums to one, and a uniform penalty across the grid cancels under normalization. Utility is also proposed to resist the empty outcome. Whether this resistance and the inability to rest at \(-\theta_L\) are one mechanism or two remains open. A derivation must therefore identify how this resistance acts on the states and read-out, as well as how the cut dynamics accommodate the discontinuity at the upper boundary (§5.4, item 7).

The design permits the cut to fall anywhere in the domain on which the entry is true. Lexicalized cuts are proposed to be more readily accessible, while an arbitrary unlexicalized cut would require additional effort. A reading of *some* that excludes *most* as well as *all* illustrates a further possible cut, at *most*. The accessibility of such resolutions is attributed to exposure, whereas the state of the cut would be determined within the trial (§5.2.1). The mechanisms that anchor the cut and determine its accessibility remain to be specified; Conjecture II concerns the cut as an inferred state (§§5.3.2 and 5.4, item 7).

Accessibility concerns how readily a cut is reached; the proposal uses affirmability to select between the two readings in Eq. (5.5). Building on the distinction between truth and finite affirmation in §§1.8 and 5.1.4, this paper assigns affirmability to an opposition: the opposition is affirmable when one of its members can be affirmed and ignorance is absent. An entry and its kernel consequently share the opposition's affirmability status. This shared status concerns whether the opposition can be settled; the member affirmed as true may be either one. This opposition-level definition is a commitment of the proposal. Exhaustification is then stipulated to hold if and only if affirmability holds.

On this account, focus on *some* and the partitive *some of the* each entail affirmation of the predicate's content (§1.1). For a partitive, the whole denoted by the definite defines the scale within which both affirmation and exhaustification are evaluated; where the size of this whole is not known, the largest size under consideration bounds the scale, as the third observation below illustrates. An affirmed *some* takes the exhaustive reading with \(\theta_A<\theta_L\): the *all*–*not all* opposition is affirmable, and *not all* is the member affirmed. Note that affirmation of *not all* is a consequence of this exhaustive interpretation. The selection condition concerns affirmability of the opposition, so the account does not require *not all* to be affirmed in advance. Grammatical exhaustification likewise makes the negation of the relevant alternative an entailment of the exhaustified sentence (Chierchia et al., 2012, pp. 2300, 2304; §1.4.1). Without focus or the partitive, the content need not be affirmed, and the reading with \(\theta_A=\theta_L\) remains available. In this case, neither member of the opposition is affirmed.

The acceptable continuation *my sister ate some of the apples, and maybe all of them* presents an objection to treating the partitive as sufficient for an exhaustive reading. The proposed response evaluates affirmability at the predicate level. If the utterance is read as *[My sister ate [some of the apples, and maybe all of them]]*, the coordinated argument already expresses ignorance when affirmability is evaluated. Under this stipulated reading, affirmability is absent and exhaustification is blocked. Implementing this selection principle would also require specifying stress at the input. The present architecture receives the utterance as text and has no representation of exhaustification or of the difference between stressed and unstressed *some*. In the proposed extension, stress would be supplied as an input property, since inferring prosody from text lies outside the present specification.

The proposal further distinguishes affirmed content from presupposed and inferred content. Suppose that admission to the common ground requires prior affirmation, and that the support of the base world prior represents the context set.[^commonground] Applied to scalar content, the proposed equivalence between affirmability and exhaustification then requires exhaustivity over a presupposed statement. An asserted statement can remain unaffirmed, so a posterior inference need not be exhaustive. This distinction is motivated by three observations considered here as the author's judgements, without experimental testing. First, negation can target the enriched reading in *my sister did not eat SOME of the apples* without an explanatory connective, consistent with the availability of enriched readings under negation discussed by Chierchia et al. (2012, p. 2305). By comparison, the author's presupposition-denial example uses an explanatory continuation: *my sister did not eat some of the apples, because I have no sister*. Second, withdrawing the affirmed exhaustive reading calls for an explicit revision in *some of the students passed; in fact all of the students passed*.

The third observation concerns the distinction between a presupposed scale and its resolution. After *I don't know if John has three or four lawyers*, *three of John's lawyers* is felicitous (Marty, 2019, p. 152). The proposed explanation takes the larger cardinality, four, to bound the presupposed scale, without a commitment that John has four lawyers; whether John's lawyers reach this bound is the resolution, which remains unaffirmed. Chierchia's (2010, p. 135) definite denotes a maximal individual, and this individual differs between the two possibilities, so the bound across them is this paper's addition. The intended analysis places exhaustification over presupposition and treats the proper-part interpretation as an entailment relative to the scale: the numeral names less than the top of the scale. This adapts Barker's (1998) treatment, on which the whole denoted by the definite is larger than the named part. Relative to a bound, the entailment does not extend to John's lawyers, so the account does not predict that John has more than three lawyers, an inference that Marty (2019, p. 151) reports as absent in such a context. Marty instead uses the example to argue for a weaker felicity condition and a presuppositional competition account (§1.1).

### 5.2.4 Motivations from the design: locality and complexity

Beyond the proposed interpretation of alternatives, the binary design has consequences for learning and search. For learning, the relevant result is the locality of the existing gain update for a fixed pair. For search, the question is whether successive binary cuts can reduce the number of levels needed for resolution. The arguments share the restriction to one opposition per level but concern different properties of the construction. For a fixed complementary pair, one utility direction suffices (nb appdx. C §2). At \(m=1\), the right-hand side of Eq. (3.10) reduces to the product \(\varphi_u\langle\varepsilon_S,b\rangle\). Under the single-projection interpretation of §3.3, both factors are available at the utility unit, so the gain update satisfies local plasticity (nb appdx. B, *Locality*). The design uses this result to motivate one cut per level and one gain for the architecture. Note that the restriction to binary oppositions is a representational choice, whereas Bogacz's locality constraints specify which information an update may use (§2.3.2). The restriction resembles binary Merge in limiting how many objects an operation combines; its relevance to plasticity here follows from the single utility direction available for a fixed pair.

Extending the locality result to the cascade requires the different solutions of an alternative to be positions of one cut, considered one at a time. Holding all these solutions together as entries would change the dimensionality problem. A derivation must show that one column serves the pair as its cut moves and that the composed maps preserve the required availability of signals (§5.4, items 2–4, 6–7). Separate gains for the utility units and local equalization of untied gains remain other possible routes (§3.4). The transfer of the latter mechanism to units with different profiles has not been tested.

The complexity motivation concerns how many alternatives a level considers and how many levels resolution requires. Predictive-processing tractability depends in part on the number of competing hypotheses at each level (Kwisthout & van Rooij, 2020; §2.3.2). Bogacz's locality constraints place no bound on this number or on the depth of a cascade. If scalar resolution is treated as a search by successive exclusions, lexical content first removes a region and pragmatic strengthening removes part of the remaining domain. For \(n+1\) distinguishable states, a cascade of balanced binary cuts would require \(O(\log n)\) levels, provided that the cuts remain balanced at every depth and each level performs bounded work. The present inventory \(\langle\textit{no},\textit{some},\textit{all}\rangle\) supplies endpoint cuts that leave the interior unresolved, so it does not instantiate the proposed saving. An inventory including *most*, assigned a cut at the log-odds midpoint, would provide a first test involving a balanced interior cut.

### 5.2.5 Motivations from the evaluation: the asymmetry and consistent strengthening

The evaluation provides two further motivations for the alternatives level: the relation between adjective antonyms and the conditions for consistent scalar strengthening. The adjective results motivate a cut that can vary independently of the lexical threshold. At a common threshold, the present architecture assigns complementary lexical regions to a maximum adjective and its minimum antonym (§5.1.3). The gaps in Experiment 2's mean judgements and the individual cut patterns motivate reconsidering this relation (§4.2.6). Relating these responses to lexical truth regions requires the stated linking assumptions: the aggregate comparison treats mean judgements as truth values, covers 11 shape and 12 artefact image sets, and, under shapes, obtains its gap almost entirely from the 23 participants identified in §4.2.6. The additional cut \(\theta_A\) offers two possible ways of changing the representation.

In the first placement, \(\theta_L\) fixes one endpoint and \(\theta_A\) fixes the other. A minimum adjective would be true on \((\theta_A,\theta_L]\), with \(\theta_A\) near the zero end of its own ordering. Separate determination of the two cuts permits the gap configuration described in §5.1.5. Because the boundaries would no longer be fixed by one threshold, the equal representability of the two endpoint regions established for the present architecture would need to be reconsidered. A derivation would need to establish the resulting asymmetry and determine whether this placement also supports consistent strengthening.

In the second placement, both lexical cuts remain at \(-\theta_L\) and \(+\theta_L\), and \(\theta_A\) lies between them. The alternative would then restrict the domain within the lexical boundaries, providing a proposed route to strengthening that does not depend solely on the base world prior. The inclusive reading at \(\theta_A=\theta_L\) still admits *all* (§5.2.3), so the effect depends on where the cut settles. This placement retains the reflection between the positions of the lexical cuts, although Conjecture II no longer gives a minimum adjective the lexically excluded zero cell of the present entry (§5.3.2). Conjecture II adds the proposed difference in endpoint behaviour: the alternative cut could rest at one but only approach zero (§5.3.2). This behaviour rests on the premise added in §5.1.5. The choice between the two placements is open, and neither has derived dynamics.

The quantifier evaluation supplies the second empirical motivation: obtaining the implicature and producing consistent strengthening are different achievements. At \(Z=6\), mode position supports H1 over the tested configurations in its domain (§4.1.7). The settled field determines a mode, subject to the additional operation of locating this mode (§3.6). Shift criteria instead compare the settled belief with the constructed literal baseline. Empirically, the utility contribution moves the mode downwards when the literal mode is near the cell of *all* and upwards elsewhere. Across the concentration–strength plane, sharpening the base world prior lowers the floors of the shift criteria while raising those of the position criteria, for both mode and mass (§4.1.5).

These opposing effects motivate suppressing a region on the basis of the selected alternative. The two shift criteria respond differently to this suppression. A cut immediately below the cell of *all* moves the mode only if the literal mode lies inside this cell, which holds for one of the five priors in §4.1.4. Moving the mode under the other four would require a cut below the literal mode, for which the design supplies no motive, so the proposed extension does not supply mode shift. Under the exhaustive reading, by contrast, a cut below the cell of *all* lowers the mass on this cell whenever the literal mass is positive and the field is otherwise unchanged. Such an influence could therefore lower the mass-shift floor without raising the position floors. Note that this benefit follows nearly by construction, since excluding a cell lowers its mass. The substantive requirement is that the derived dynamics settle the cut below the cell under the exhaustive reading, and that the settled read-out then satisfy the criterion against the literal baseline. The hypothesis is specific to the present architecture and the results at \(Z=6\). Moreover, the literal listener is constructed from the base world prior and lexical semantics, so the strengthening comparison inherits these assumptions (§4.1.1).

### 5.2.6 The four questions about alternatives, and the symmetry problem

The proposed representation and its motivations can now be related to the four questions raised in §1.6. Each resolved opposition has two members: a meaning and its negation. Membership is restricted to a level, which contains one pair, while other entries belong to other levels of the cascade. Selection concerns where negation cuts the domain on which the entry is true. The proposed architecture permits several positions, with lexicalized cuts expected to be more accessible (§5.2.3). Finally, the motivation for computing an alternative comes from the fixed-pair locality result and the conditional search argument (§5.2.4). These arguments explain the proposed binary organization; deriving how a particular cut is selected requires the dynamics specified as an open task in §5.4.

This organization changes how the proposal encounters the symmetry problem. In Katzir's (2007, pp. 672–674) discussion, *all* and *some but not all* are competing stronger alternatives to *some*. Given the truth of *some*, both cannot be false. In the proposed binary resolution, by contrast, the resolved meaning and its kernel constitute the pair; the two members are not two alternatives to be excluded together. Within the domain of *some*, *not all* is the kernel of *all*. The two cases of the single cut in Eq. (5.5) yield the intended readings of *some*: a cut below \(+\theta_L\) excludes the cell of *all* and gives the exhaustive reading, and a cut at \(+\theta_L\) leaves the opposition unresolved and admits *all*. The second case corresponds to Katzir's (2007, p. 673) outcome, on which the speaker is not opinionated about either alternative; the proposal retains this outcome as the reading of ignorance (§5.2.3). Cardinality and the kernel relation remove the contradiction between two denied alternatives, but they leave a question of direction: denying *some but not all* would yield *all*. Eq. (5.5) stipulates the orientation, since the retained region lies below the cut and the exhaustive reading therefore affirms *not all*. The design does not yet derive why the retained region is not the cell of *all* (§5.4, item 7).

The empty region at the lower lexical boundary raises a separate question about consistency. At high lexical strength, the proposed restriction keeps alternative resolution within the domain left by the lexical entry; at lower strength, lexical exclusion is defeasible and the cut may cross the lower lexical boundary (§§3.2 and 5.2.3). Preservation of the lexical domain would prevent readmission of excluded degrees, but a region that is false everywhere would still contradict an asserted existential reading if the region were settled. Domain containment therefore does not answer the symmetry problem. On the proposal, the cut approaches this position without resting there (§5.3.2), so the empty region is not a reading of *some*. Affirmability offers the other proposed response to the symmetry problem. Failure to affirm *not all* can leave *all* available without affirming *all*, because absence of affirmation differs from Boolean negation (§5.1.4). The proposal uses this distinction to characterize the ignorance reading. Its application requires the opposition-level selection mechanism of §5.2.3; ordinary Boolean denial of *not all* would continue to entail *all*. The dynamical account must therefore specify how the alternatives level represents an unresolved opposition as well as a resolved one (§5.4, item 7).

### 5.2.7 Beside Katzir's structural alternatives

Katzir (2007, pp. 678–679) generates alternatives through finite sequences of deletions, contractions and replacements by constituents of the same category. The initial substitution source is the lexicon; the final definition also includes subtrees of the utterance, with nearby structures considered in the accompanying discussion (pp. 685–686). The structural procedure produces a set whose size is not fixed at two. For a compound expression, substitutions at several constituents can contribute candidates to this set. The present proposal instead resolves one alternative at a level, with other possible resolutions represented by other positions of the cut.

The accounts also differ in how lexical material enters the construction. In the proposed cascade, the alternative is a kernel whether or not a word lexicalizes this kernel. For *bent*, the partner would be *not bent*, which Conjecture III distinguishes from *straight* (§5.3.3). Katzir's procedure admits *straight* as a same-category substitution. Both accounts nevertheless allow alternatives beyond a fixed list of lexical competitors: Katzir's enriched source admits available complex constituents, while the proposed cut can occupy an unlexicalized position. The latter possibility is assigned an accessibility cost in the proposal; Katzir's structural membership rule does not itself specify an equivalent processing cost.

The case of *most* makes the comparison more specific. The same lexical-substitution operation that admits *all* in place of *some* can admit *most*. In Katzir's account, using this candidate additionally depends on the strength and weak-assertability conditions described in §1.6; candidate membership alone does not establish a default *not most* inference. The present proposal instead treats exclusion at *most* as a possible resolution of the one cut, obtainable with effort (§5.2.3). How the proposed accessibility mechanism distinguishes this lexicalized cut from the cut associated with *all* remains to be specified, and the comparison is not evaluated here. Thus, Katzir supplies a structural criterion for membership in an alternative set, whereas the proposed cascade combines binary membership with graded accessibility of cut positions. A cut can be difficult to obtain without being excluded by definition. This distinction identifies a question for the proposed dynamics: how exposure and the current domain jointly determine which of the available cuts is reached. The dependence of cut placement on exposure and item properties is central to Conjecture II (§5.3.2).

[^ignorance]: The literature distinguishes a scalar implicature, on which the speaker believes the alternative with *all* to be false, from an ignorance implicature, on which the speaker is unsure whether this alternative is true (§1.1). This paper uses one variational representation, without a separate representation of the speaker's knowledge alongside the listener's. In its terms, scalar implicature excludes *all*, whereas the ignorance reading admits *all* without affirming *all* (§5.2.6).

[^commonground]: The proposed correspondence takes the context set representing the common ground to be the support of the base world prior (§1.4.3).

## 5.3 The four conjectures

The proposed design makes cut placement a question about the interaction between lexical representation and inference. The four conjectures develop the proposed relations among atomicity, scalar standards, antonymy and lexicalization.

### 5.3.1 Conjecture I: atomicity and lexical strength

Conjecture I states that predicates with unstable atomicity have weaker lexical strength. Atomicity follows Chierchia's (2010) distinction between stable and unstable atoms (§1.8); in the architecture, the number of atoms resolved is \(n\), and lexical strength is \(\Lambda\). The conjecture concerns how stably a predicate fixes its atoms, rather than the value of \(n\) alone. On the position of §4.2.1, gradable adjectives have unstable atoms, so the conjecture's comparison is with predicates whose atoms are stable, such as count nouns (§1.8). The parameter sweep varies \(n\) between runs, while each run holds the lexical field fixed, so this sweep does not measure the stability at issue (§4.2.1). The broader proposal is that, for predicates with unstable atomicity, \(\theta_L\) would be learned as the generative scalar mapping the lexical field to word form (nb appdx. A, Eqs. A2 and A4). The parameter enters twice: as the decoder's gain and as the threshold defining \(\chi_y\). Equation (A4) differentiates through the gain alone. Learning the boundary would therefore require specifying the threshold dependence as well as unclamping the lexical field (§3.3).

The fitted parameters nevertheless show where the two classes differ in the present implementation. Within the searched range, the maximum-class fits provide no upper bound on either \(n\) or \(\Lambda\). The minimum-class full-network fit instead occurs at the smallest sampled \(n\), namely four, with \(\Lambda=23.11\) and \(R^2=0.456\) (§4.2.2). The finite lexical-strength estimate is specific to the full network: for the separately fitted literal listener, the fit is flat over \(\Lambda\) values from 32 to 128. Because the maximum-class score is flat from \(\Lambda=16\) upwards, the absence of an upper bound does not show that the maximum class has stronger lexical strength than the minimum class (§4.2.2). Given the poor minimum-class fit and the absence of a measure of atomic stability, these estimates leave Conjecture I undecided. The decline in judged truth towards the minimum adjective's zero also suggests a cut displaced from this endpoint (§4.2.6). Under shapes, this decline is largely due to the 23 participants identified in §4.2.6. Relating this displacement to unstable atomicity would require a model in which the lexical field and the quantities determining \(n\) can change, together with a comparison that includes the relative class.

### 5.3.2 Conjecture II: the alternative cut and the scale classes

Conjecture II states that, in an adjective's own ordering, its truth region extends from an alternative cut to the top of the scale. The classes differ in the location of the cut: just above zero for minimum adjectives, in the interior for relative adjectives, and at one for maximum adjectives. An interior cut could have a role comparable to the proposed cut for *most*. The proposed form is \((\theta_A,\theta_L]\): the lower cut is open, in agreement with the affirmative region of §5.1.4, and the upper bound retains the top cell under the convention of §5.2.3. At \(\theta_A=\theta_L\), the same convention retains the full cell of *all*, \([\theta_L,Z]\), rather than a singleton grid point, so a maximum adjective's region contains its boundary, in agreement with the closed region of §5.1.4. Reflecting a bounded reading of *some* likewise turns an upper alternative cut into a lower cut, although the reflected region includes this cut and excludes its upper lexical endpoint (§5.3.4). The comparison therefore concerns the orientation of the movable cut: on a shared scale, the minimum adjective takes the lower-cut form and its maximum antonym the corresponding upper-cut form.

The conjecture is stated under the second placement in §5.2.5: the lexical cuts remain at \(-\theta_L\) and \(+\theta_L\), while \(\theta_A\) is a state inferred within the trial. The class-to-entry map evaluated in §4.2 is a fixed-entry approximation to this form. The map gives a minimum adjective the entry of *some*, which excludes the zero cell lexically. The conjecture instead takes a minimum adjective's lexical entry to leave the zero cell unexcluded and the alternative cut to exclude this cell; as \(\theta_A\) approaches \(-\theta_L\), the region approaches the map's region. The conjecture thus asks how inference over the alternative cut could generate the class differences. For a minimum adjective, the alternative cut would lie near \(-\theta_L\), at the end involved in the mixed pair's flip relation. The proposed asymmetry is that the cut could approach \(-\theta_L\), the boundary of the cell representing zero, without resting there, whereas a cut could rest at \(+\theta_L\). Such behaviour would connect the boundary of the first candidate with the independently determined cut of the second (§§5.1.4–5.1.5). This connection rests on the premise added in §5.1.5; no derived dynamics yet establish the endpoint behaviour.

The cut's location must be determined by the quantities carried by the item: \(n\), \(\Lambda\), the base world prior and the entry. The proposed inference would receive no additional class label selecting a resting point. One possible mechanism is attraction towards the lexical cuts, increasing with lexical strength. Only \(+\theta_L\) is proposed as a resting point: \(-\theta_L\) would attract the cut without holding the cut.[^attractor] A further term would be needed to support a stable interior position. The finite-variance prior required for a new state direction provides one candidate (§5.2.2). If lexical attraction dominates, the cut would rest at \(+\theta_L\) or approach \(-\theta_L\); if the additional term dominates, the cut could settle in the interior and move with the base world prior. The latter possibility is motivated by the context dependence of relative standards (Kennedy & McNally, 2005, p. 361). The proposed terms must also account for the difference between approaching zero and resting at one, since attraction to two endpoints alone does not establish this difference.

Two options for the resulting dynamics remain open. Under option (a), each item's quantities determine one settled cut, and the classes correspond to different regimes of the same landscape. The determinacy adopted in §3.0 would then hold item by item, even if the existing concavity proof did not extend. Listeners with the same item quantities would reach the same cut; variation in these quantities between listeners could produce graded mean truth judgements from sharp individual responses. This option motivates examining whether an item's individual cuts concentrate around one location. Experiment 2 provides a limited check of the proposed concentration of individual cuts. After setting aside the 23 participants who frequently selected only the top image for minimum and relative adjectives, the test does not reject unimodality for any item; with these participants included, the minimum responses show two concentrations (§4.2.6). The remaining distributions are consistent with option (a). However, relating the observed concentration to a unique resting point also requires an account of the variation in listeners' quantities. The top-image responses allow two interpretations. Participants may have understood the task as requesting the best-fitting image, in which case the responses address a different judgement. Alternatively, their quantities may place the cut at \(+\theta_L\) across the classes they judged. Option (a) allows the latter possibility through differences between listeners. The response patterns do not decide between these interpretations.

Under option (b), an item's quantities permit several resting points, and the starting state determines which one is reached. This starting state would have to depend on the item or trial, rather than being assigned by class. Listeners with the same item quantities could then settle at different cuts, allowing a graded mean judgement to arise from several groups of sharp individual judgements. This option permits more than one concentration of cuts without predicting their number. A unique settled cut would no longer follow from the item's quantities alone, so adopting option (b) would revise the methodological preference for determinacy in §3.0. Distinguishing the two options therefore requires predictions about how variation between listeners or trials affects the resulting cuts.

The dynamics must also determine whether the lexical cuts bound \(\theta_A\) throughout resolution. The proposal is that high lexical strength resists crossing either boundary, while lower strength permits crossing. At the lower boundary, this resistance is intended to preserve the region excluded by the lexical entry (§5.2.3). The endpoint-cell convention specifies the represented regions and their discontinuity; the objective must additionally establish that the cut can rest at \(+\theta_L\) and not at \(-\theta_L\), and whether the cut crosses either boundary. Deriving these dynamics would make the cut mechanism precise enough to compare with affirmative access and to determine whether the two candidates of §5.1 describe one mechanism or distinct mechanisms.

### 5.3.3 Conjecture III: antonyms share a scale without partitioning it

Conjecture III states that a minimum adjective and its maximum antonym share a scale without partitioning it. An entry and its kernel, \(\{\chi,\ker\chi\}\), partition the represented domain by construction (§3.4). Antonymy need not impose this relation. The literature on absolute antonyms is divided on how to characterize their relation (§1.7.3).[^regions] In the present architecture, the mixed pair's complementary regions follow from a common \(\theta_L\); the conjecture proposes to relax this commitment (§5.1.3). The departure from complementarity in Experiment 2's aggregate truth judgements and the related cut comparisons motivates the conjecture (§4.2.6). Whether two regions partition a domain can be specified on a set of degrees, making this question independent of the stronger scale-identity criteria considered in §5.1.1. The two candidates provide different possible departures from a partition: affirmative interiors can omit a boundary, while separately placed cuts can leave an intervening interval. The omitted boundary has no width, so only separately placed cuts can supply the observed gaps, and these cuts require the appropriate ordering. Under Conjecture II, this ordering holds whenever a minimum adjective's cut lies above \(-\theta_L\): the maximum antonym retains the cell representing the minimum adjective's zero, and the interval between \(-\theta_L\) and the cut falls under neither adjective. Determining what fixes the observed gap widths remains part of the derivation (§5.4, item 7).

### 5.3.4 Conjecture IV: the O corner

The O corner, *not all*, lacks a single-word lexicalization in the pattern reviewed in §1.7.2. The present architecture nevertheless represents *some* and *not all* equally: their endpoint exclusions are reflections, and *not all* is the kernel of *all* (§5.1.3). Conjecture IV locates a possible explanation for the lexicalization difference in whether a literal reading can be settled. The reflection must preserve the endpoint conventions introduced in §5.2.3. A resolved *some* region has an open lower lexical boundary and an inclusive upper alternative cut. Reflection exchanges these roles: the alternative cut becomes the lower bound, while the upper lexical bound is open. Writing \(\theta_A\) for the lower cut in this reflected representation gives \([\theta_A,\theta_L)\). Note that the open upper bound excludes the cell of *all*, as the O-corner kernel requires. The adjective region in Conjecture II, \((\theta_A,\theta_L]\), is open at its lower cut and retains the top cell in the adjective's own ordering; the two forms therefore share a movable lower cut but differ at both ends. At \(\theta_A=-\theta_L\), the mirrored endpoint-cell convention retains the entire cell of *none*, giving the literal O-corner region \([-Z,\theta_L)\).

The conjecture combines the proposed asymmetry in cut dynamics with a requirement on lexicalization. Here settlement concerns the cut: a cut could reach a stable position while the opposition remains unresolved. For *some*, this position is \(\theta_A=+\theta_L\), where the lexical region retains *all* (§5.2.3). The reflected *not all* region admits *none* only at the lower endpoint position. If the cut can rest at one but only approach zero, then a literal reading can be settled for *some* but not for *not all*. Conjecture IV proposes that an entry of the quantifier type requires a settled literal reading to be lexicalized, whereas an adjective does not. A minimum adjective could consequently be lexicalized despite a cut that remains above zero. The shared feature is the proposed behaviour of the lower cut; the adjective and O-corner regions retain the different boundaries specified above.

Horn's account distinguishes the positive and negative corners and favours the less complex positive form when the alternatives convey the same information (§1.7.2). Conjecture IV instead proposes to derive the preference for *some* from the asymmetry between the two endpoints. For this explanation to succeed, the model must determine which end is zero independently of the words that happen to be lexicalized. Defining zero from the ordering of an already lexicalized entry would leave the direction of explanation unresolved. An independently defined orientation would fix which endpoint the proposed asymmetry concerns. An acquisition mechanism must then explain how this asymmetry shapes the lexicon. Under Conjecture IV, failure to settle a literal quantifier reading must prevent acquisition. The present architecture stipulates the inventory and clamps the lexical field, leaving this mechanism to be developed. The acquisition account must also explain why the requirement applies to quantifiers but not adjectives, given their shared scalar format in the proposal. Conjecture I may bear on the distinction, but the connection has not been derived.[^countmeasure] The account must also explain why the complex expression *not all* remains available and whether the same mechanism extends to the corresponding gaps among connectives and modal verbs (§1.7.2).

## 5.4 What remains to be derived

The preceding arguments identify what the additional level must represent and which phenomena its dynamics should explain. A complete construction must connect this representation to the objective, local updates and read-out. Let \(\varphi_a\) retain its meaning as the real-valued log-odds state of the alternatives level (§5.2.2), and let \(\chi_D\) identify the domain selected by its sign. The following eight requirements organize the derivation and the points cited earlier in this section. Completing these derivations would allow a comparison of the proposed alternatives level with the existing architecture and a test of whether the added mechanisms explain the patterns that motivated the extension.

**1. The reference point.** The affine involution in Eq. (5.3) needs a reference point on each restricted domain. The coordinate convention must also specify how the sign of \(\varphi_a\) selects the domain represented by \(\chi_D\).

**2. The lexical prediction.** The new form of \(g_L\) and its Jacobian must specify how the additional level changes predictions at the lexical level. These expressions determine which activities enter the corresponding state and error updates.

**3. Exclusion coding.** The construction must establish whether the alternatives level represents excluded content in the same sense as the lexical level. Applying the existing exclusion-coding argument on a restricted domain requires showing how this argument is instantiated at the new level.

**4. Errors, variances and convergence.** The new error unit and its variance must be specified, together with the conditions under which the composed state maps remain affine and the objective remains strictly concave at fixed parameters. The possible non-concavity associated with the alternative cut requires a separate analysis (§§5.2.2 and 5.3.2).

**5. The terminating prior.** With one utility dimension, \(\mu_u\) is a single real value. If exposure contains one complementary pair, the lexical contribution cancels from the aggregate drive (§4.2.5). Under the existing gain construction, a finite learned gain therefore requires both nonzero \(\mu_u\) and a nonzero projection of the base world prior on the utility column. The proposed level must specify a terminating prior consistent with this condition.

**6. The utility direction.** One column separates a fixed complementary pair on one domain (nb appdx. C §2). The cascade requires a definition of the domain of this column and the contrast it carries as the alternatives units represent different oppositions. The derivation must establish how the proposed single utility direction and gain operate across these domains while preserving the locality result of §5.2.4.

**7. The alternative-cut dynamics.** The objective must specify the terms involving \(\theta_A\), including any attraction towards the lexical cuts and any term supporting an interior resting point. The derivation must choose or compare the two placements in §5.2.5, determine whether the lexical boundaries constrain the cut throughout inference, and establish whether an item's quantities yield one settled cut or several. The proposed difference between approaching zero and resting at one must follow from these terms. The analysis must also address the discontinuous endpoint-cell convention and whether the proposed resistance from utility to the empty truth region at \(\theta_A=-\theta_L\) and the proposed inability to rest there are one mechanism or two. Because the normalized read-out remains a probability distribution, the account must specify how this resistance is expressed in the states and read-out (§5.2.3).

The same dynamics must distinguish a resolved opposition from the unresolved reading at \(\theta_A=+\theta_L\), and explain how affirmability selects between them. The derivation must establish that the exhaustive reading settles the cut below the cell of *all* (§5.2.5), and must derive the orientation that Eq. (5.5) stipulates, on which the retained region lies below the cut (§5.2.6). This includes determining whether the restricted-domain involution contributes to the proposed endpoint asymmetry. Relating the resulting process to Vickers's (1989) account would then show whether affirmative access and independent cut placement provide one explanation or two (§5.1).

The objective must also relate the level's two quantities. \(\varphi_a\) is the belief in one member of an opposition against the other, whereas \(\theta_A\) is the position at which the opposition divides the scale; the design has not specified whether both are states or how one constrains the other. A hard indicator gives no gradient in \(\theta_A\) between grid nodes, so ascent requires a soft form of the cut. The open and inclusive bounds of §§5.2.3 and 5.3.2 would then describe the limit of this form, and its width would be a further quantity. The retention of the cell of *all* at \(+\theta_L\) needs separate treatment, since a soft step at \(+\theta_L\) excludes this cell. The update of \(\theta_A\) must also be shown to be local. Bogacz's (2017, Eq. 12) update lets a state unit weight a received error by a function of its own activity. The unit for \(\theta_A\) would receive the errors of several grid nodes, each weighted by a function of \(\theta_A\) and of that node's fixed position, and each node would require \(\theta_A\) to form its prediction. Whether these connections satisfy the constraints of §2.3 remains to be shown.

**8. Lexicalization.** Conjecture IV additionally requires a mechanism of acquisition, a reason for the quantifier–adjective difference, an independently defined orientation, and an explanation of complex *not all* and the possible extension to connectives and modals (§5.3.4).

# 6. Discussion and conclusion

The evaluations and proposed extension raise two questions about the account of scalar resolution: which patterns the present representation explains, and what would be required for the specified process to be realizable. The scale-class comparison bears on the first question. The dynamics, stopping rules and possible role of computational cost bear on the second.

## 6.1 Interpretation of the scale-class comparison

The maximum-class results show how much the stipulated endpoint representation can account for without inferring a threshold. Both the literal listener and the full network reach \(R^2=0.993\), and every tested ladder point with \(\Lambda\ge16\) gives 0.992 for both read-outs at every sampled \(n\) (§4.2.2). The broad plateau is consistent with the hard-entry limit: the fit constrains lexical strength from below within the search but does not identify the atom count. This agreement must be interpreted together with partition R1, which assigns the degree-zero image the endpoint cell by stipulation (§4.2.1). The comparison therefore evaluates the predictions of an endpoint-anchored representation; the fit does not independently establish where the threshold belongs.

The minimum-class comparison shows a discrepancy in the contribution of utility across image types. Under artefacts, the full network reaches \(R^2=0.879\) and places the mean scale position only 0.03 above the data. Under shapes, the score is 0.094 and the mean is 0.65 too high (§4.2.4). Relative to the tempered control, utility moves the mean upwards by 0.58 for shapes and 0.83 for artefacts, whereas the data lie −0.08 and +0.80 from this control (§4.2.5). Thus, the full network closely matches the artefact mean, while the shape mean lies near the tempered control. The utility contribution varies through the represented inputs, but the architecture has no separate mechanism that suppresses this contribution according to image type.

The elicited prior is a candidate source of the discrepancy because it is the model input that differs between the two image conditions at fixed class parameters. For minimum adjectives, the elicited prior places 0.923 of its mass at position 1 under shapes, compared with 0.461 under artefacts. The observed departure from the empirical literal listener is also smaller under shapes, +0.30 against +0.78 (§4.2.6; nb appdx. F §§4, 9). If individual choices tend towards the mode of a participant's belief, pooling single choices could overstate the concentration of the operative base world prior (§2.5). However, replacing the elicited prior with a uniform one does not repair the shape fit at the same lexical parameters. The score falls from 0.094 to 0.069, and the full network's mean position rises from 4.25 to 4.58, further above the observed 3.59 (nb appdx. F §10). The uniform prior is motivated by Xiang et al.'s (2022) slider task, but this control gives no support to the proposed correction through reduced concentration. The cause of the mismatch remains open: the two image conditions may differ in ways that the elicited prior does not record.

The contrast between classes also clarifies the role assigned to semantic convention. Xiang et al. (2022) argue that Bayesian reasoning must be combined with conventions about thresholds. In the present architecture, endpoint orientation is supplied by the scale representation and class-to-entry map: the entries are anchored in the Voronoi cells of counts zero and \(n\), and \(n\) fixes \(\theta_L\) (§§3.2–3.3 and 4.2.1). Once these commitments are made, the boundary is not a further fitted threshold. Because Xiang et al.'s models specify thresholds differently, their fit statistics do not provide a direct comparative test of this architecture. The architectural comparison instead identifies what the stipulated scale structure reproduces without threshold inference, particularly the maximum-class profile, and where further specification is needed, particularly for minimum adjectives and the relation between antonyms. The fitted parameters retain the conditions of this comparison. The pair \((n,\Lambda)\) is fitted separately for each class and contains the only fitted quantities in this paper. The results depend on the three linking stipulations and partition R1 (§4.2.1). Within the score band of 0.005 in \(R^2\), only the minimum class's full-network \(\Lambda\) is bounded on both sides; its \(n\) lies at the lower end of the sampled ladder, and the maximum-class bands reach the search boundaries. The minimum fit also changes with the grid half-width, chiefly below \(Z=6\) (§4.2.2). These features limit how precisely the parameters can be inferred from the observed profiles.

The antonym comparison raises a distinct representational issue. At a common \(\theta_L\), the lexical flip gives complementary regions for a maximum adjective and its minimum antonym. Experiment 2's aggregate judgements and individual cut distributions depart from this relation under the linking assumptions discussed in §4.2.6. The departure is larger for artefacts; much of the aggregate shape gap is associated with the 23 participants whose responses frequently select only the top image. §5.1 distinguishes affirmative access, which omits a boundary of no width, from independently placed cuts, which can leave a gap of positive width, and §5.2 develops an alternatives level that could represent the latter. The proposed connection between the two explanations remains to be established through the cut dynamics.

## 6.2 Realizability, halting, and plausibility

The stopping question arises on two timescales. Slow plasticity changes the gain across exposures, while fast inference settles the states for a fixed gain. Under the learning conditions of §3.3, the slow flow approaches \(\theta_u^\ast\) as the objective flattens, without reaching the asymptote in finite time. The objective supplies no earlier stopping event (nbtc 3 §8.5). Reporting the stationary point therefore leaves a question about where an implemented or biological learning process would stop.

The simulation stops slow learning when the magnitude of the gain update falls below a chosen tolerance. At \(m=1\), this magnitude is locally readable where the gain is applied. At the implemented \(m=2\), however, the update combines contributions that no single unit receives (§3.4). The fast loop has a separate locality problem: its tolerance is set using the global spectral quantity \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\). Both tolerances require a choice that the objective does not determine. Tolerance-based halting is consequently a proposed direction for the architecture, with the information access and tolerance value still requiring an account (§3.4; nbtc 3 §8.5). Bogacz's (2017, §2.4) learning example helps locate the role of the exposure procedure. Parameters receive nonidentical observations and continue changing, although their expected update can vanish. Batching the present uniform ensemble instead produces the monotonic slow flow considered here. This difference explains why the simulation can be analysed in terms of approach to one stationary gain. A process account still needs to explain how learning and inference stop or continue under the relevant exposure conditions.

The gain matters because further learning can increase inference cost after the interpretation criteria are already satisfied. In the configuration examined in §4.1.6, mode position and both conjunctions hold early and remain satisfied at the subsequently checked gains up to \(\theta_u^\ast\). Meanwhile, the critical-damping constraint requires increasing separation between the state and error timescales as \(\theta_u^2\) grows, and the implemented integrator requires more steps. The asymptote is reported because the configuration fixes its value without a stopping tolerance, rather than because a finite trajectory reaches it. The required timescale separation is an architectural plausibility question; the step counts quantify the cost through the particular simulation (§4.1.6; nb appdx. G). An alternatives-level construction would need to address this tension, for example through a reachable slow optimum or criteria whose satisfaction does not depend on an increasingly costly gain.

## 6.3 Cost in the dynamics

A stopping rule can respond to numerical convergence without representing the cost of computation. The simulation's tolerance rules do so: neither the fast nor the slow dynamics contains a cost term that determines when to stop. Pragmatic accounts can give cost a different role. In Relevance Theory, the balance of cognitive effect and effort guides how far interpretation proceeds, and the hearer adopts a satisfactory interpretation (Sperber & Wilson, 1995, as cited in Bott & Noveck, 2004, p. 439). Incorporating such a role into the present account would require specifying how the system represents the cost relevant to continuing an inference.

General limits on computation help frame this question. Turing (1936, p. 248) establishes that no general procedure decides whether an arbitrary machine ever prints a specified symbol. Wolpert (2008, §3.3) considers inference devices abstractly, through a setup and a binary conclusion. A device weakly infers a quantity when, for each yes-or-no probe of the quantity, some setup guarantees a correct answer. Applied to the device's own conclusion, the negation probe would require the conclusion to equal its own negation. No device can satisfy this requirement. The restriction does not depend on particular physical laws and excludes complete self-inference in this formal sense. Neither result establishes that every estimate of a system's own computational cost is impossible; the question for the present architecture is which cost information its operations make available.[^selfref]

The cost considered here is a functional of the completed inference trajectory, including its duration or number of steps. For this deterministic simulation, the configuration and initial state fix the trajectory in advance, and an analyst can derive its cost scaling or calculate a realized cost by running the process (nb appdx. G). The network nevertheless has no operation that computes, reads or anticipates this full cost during inference. A current-state quantity can influence an update without providing such a computation. Adding a cost estimate would therefore require a representational and dynamical specification, including how the estimate is obtained.

Two approaches to bounded rationality distinguish an analyst's cost assessment from the computation being assessed. Russell and Subramanian (1995, pp. 576, 578–579) question the application of Good's type II rationality separately to each decision: selecting the best deliberation net of its cost can itself demand expensive metalevel computation. Their bounded-optimality criterion instead evaluates programs relative to an architecture's resources and task environment (pp. 575–576, 583). The design criterion does not require the running agent to solve this optimization afresh at each decision. Griffiths et al. (2015, pp. 223–225; see also Lieder & Griffiths, 2020) use expected utility net of computational cost as a resource-rational description. The analyst specifies a family of computations and their costs, then asks which computation makes best use of the available resources. Evolution, development and learning are proposed sources of this adaptation; whether and how the mind learns to allocate resources optimally remains a further question. Neither analytical criterion alone specifies an internal cost estimator.

The same distinction applies to adding cost to the present dynamics. A stipulated term would initially serve as a proxy for trajectory cost. Once the term changes the dynamics, the cost to be predicted is the cost of the modified process. If \(C\) denotes the proposed cost, exact agreement would require the self-consistency condition

\[
C=\operatorname{cost}(\text{dynamics given }C).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Equation (6.1) identifies what an exact cost term would have to satisfy. The present network contains no mechanism for finding this fixed point. Whether an anticipatory mechanism could supply an exact value is left open. Accounts of self-reference suggest different ways to formulate such a mechanism. Rosen (1985, pp. 8–10) describes a system acting on the prediction of an internal model of its future. The model's present prediction supplies the influence on action. Because the model is generally imperfect, Rosen calls the resulting behaviour quasi-anticipatory (p. 13). Kauffman's (2005, pp. 133–135) eigenform account instead represents the stable object of a recursion as a fixed point, \(J=F(J)\). Applying this conception to a cost-feedback process makes convergence a substantive requirement, since iteration can also oscillate. Hofstadter's (1979, Introduction) account of loops across levels provides another form of self-reference, with Gödel's construction illustrating how a statement can refer to its own provability and expose limits of the formal system. These accounts distinguish a model, a recursive fixed point and a loop across levels. A cost-feedback construction would need to specify which form of self-reference its operations realize. Abramsky et al. (2026, §4.2) likewise emphasize the role of temporal unfolding when analysing self-referential processes.

For the present architecture, two forms of proxy can be specified more concretely. A configuration-level proxy could use the condition number

\[
\kappa(H)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}
=\theta_u^2+2,
\tag{6.2}
\]

under the unit variances and orthonormal utility basis used here (nb appdx. G, Eq. G1). The gain is fixed during one inference, so this quantity is determined before this inference starts. Feeding the proxy into a quantity that determines the learned gain would create a dependency to be solved across the configuration; the equality in Eq. (6.2) does not supply this feedback rule. A second proxy could use the realized cost of completed inferences as feedback on the slow timescale. This cross-trial construction would require a halting signal and a specified update map. If the update iterates the map from a proposed cost to the cost of the resulting dynamics, and this map is a contraction on an appropriate complete domain, then iteration would converge to a fixed point satisfying Eq. (6.1). Contraction is a sufficient condition; without it, convergence requires another argument. Both proxies remain proposals, and neither is part of the reported dynamics.

## 6.4 What the algorithm specifies

The distinctions above explain why a computational goal leaves questions that an algorithm must answer. The goal alone does not specify which variables are represented or how their values are read. In this architecture, the normalized read-out \(q\) over the world coordinate and the delta distribution at the settled state \((\varphi_S^\ast,\varphi_u^\ast)\) concern different variables and require different operations (§3.6). Agreement about an optimum therefore leaves the representation and read-out to be specified. The algorithm also determines how the optimum is approached and when computation stops. The gap between the gain at which the criteria first hold and the more costly asymptote is a property of the specified dynamics (§4.1.6). A computational objective alone supplies neither this trajectory nor its stopping rule (§6.2). Resource-rational analysis incorporates computational cost by specifying a family of computations to be evaluated (§6.3). The resource analysis and the process specification thus address connected questions: assigning a cost requires identifying the computation that incurs it.

[^selfref]: The architectural argument concerns the absence of an internal cost mechanism. Deterministic trajectory cost can be fixed before execution, and the simulation's scaling is analysed externally in nb appdx. G. Neither Turing's result, Wolpert's self-inference restriction nor Gödel's incompleteness theorems establishes that an anticipated cost must be inexact. For self-reference analysed as a process unfolding in time, see Abramsky et al. (2026).

## 6.5 Limitations

### Numerical substrate

The grid truncates \(\zeta\in\mathbb R\) to \([-Z,Z]\), and every criterion of §4.1 depends on \(Z\). At \(Z=6\), H1 is supported over the tested configurations in its domain; from \(Z=7\), most domain cells provide counterexamples and so satisfy H0 (§§4.1.3, 4.1.5 and 4.1.7). The closed scale of §3.2 does not settle this dependence, because the logit coordinate is unbounded and the half-width is a further stipulation. In the scale-class comparison, the maximum-class score barely changes with \(Z\), and the minimum-class score changes chiefly below \(Z=6\) (§4.2.2). Refining \(K\) stabilizes the fields (§3.2). A criterion defined by a cut can nevertheless change when a grid node crosses \(\theta_L\), and the present runs do not establish a minimum node count (§§4.1.3 and 4.1.7). Future work should therefore separate node placement from resolution, express minimum resolution relative to \(n\) and the width of the cell of *all*, and use smooth statistics where possible. A principled grid could make \((K,Z)\) a function of \(n\), replacing two stipulated constants with a representational rule, but the present phase does not implement such a grid. On the present grid, \(\theta_L\) is no longer below \(Z=6\) from \(n=2^8\) (§4.2.1).

The simulation cost of one integrated inference is

\[
O\!\left(Km\left[m+(\theta_u^2+2)\log\frac{a}{\mathrm{tol}}\right]\right),
\tag{6.3}
\]

where \(a\) is the largest derivative at the start of integration and \(\mathrm{tol}\) is the stopping tolerance (nb appdx. G, Eq. G3). Equation (6.3) gives the complexity of one implementation of the architecture, not a general complexity bound for the architecture itself.

### Architectural limits

At fixed \(\theta_u\), the state objective has a unique maximizer, and the fast subsystem converges to this maximizer (§3.4). Learning has no corresponding guarantee, since \(\mathcal F\) is not jointly concave in \((\theta_u,\varphi_u)\). That learning reaches \(\theta_u^\ast\) is conditional on the start \(\theta_u(0)=0\), the exposure ensemble, the coupling condition and equilibration of the fast subsystem between updates; from other starts, the flow need not reach \(\theta_u^\ast\) (§§3.0 and 3.3; nb appdx. B). The linear-Gaussian design makes the recurrent dynamics testable, but the same choice makes these dynamics an implementation claim rather than a computational necessity (§3.0). At \(m=2\), the update of the one plastic parameter is not local, so the present architecture does not meet the constraint of local plasticity (§3.4). The relay tried as a repair does not remove this departure (nb appdx. B). The choice \(m=2\) is motivated by independent tilt and width coordinates, but the sufficiency of \(m=2\) is not established (§3.5), and the treatment of these coordinates in a cascade is only proposed (§5.2.2).

The architecture's scope is limited in further respects. In its current form, the architecture does not reach predicates that are both sharply bounded and multidimensional in the sense that their dimensions must be inferred jointly (nbtc 3 §9.3).[^multidim] The architecture also does not learn \(\Lambda\), \(\theta_L\), \(B\), \(\mu_u\), \(\ell_0\), the variances or the inventory (§§3.1–3.3). Because the lexical field is clamped, \(n\) cannot vary within the model; the sweep of §4.2 stands in for this variation and remains to be compared with a model in which \(n\) varies (§4.2.1). The inventory has no context threshold, so the relative class is not modelled (§4.2.1).

### Open read-out question

It remains unclear whether a read-out is part of the system and must therefore satisfy locality; §3.6 states what each read-out requires. A locally implemented mode read-out could assume a single local maximum and compare neighbouring state units. Because every audited configuration under *some* has a single local maximum (§3.6), such a read-out reproduces the reported *some* results. The read-out is nevertheless stipulated, requires new lateral access, and does not guarantee the global mode for fields with several local maxima.

## 6.6 Predictions

The implemented architecture supports several conditional predictions, each with a condition that the present evaluation leaves untested. Non-uniform exposure to scalar entries would move \(\theta_u^\ast\), although the size of this movement is not tested (§§3.4 and 5.2.1). Whether the movement changes an interpretation depends on where the gain ends. In the configurations evaluated, the settled field lies at its large-gain limit (§§4.1.3 and 4.2.5), and in the one configuration in which the onset of the criteria was measured, the criteria hold from \(|\theta_u|\) near 2 to \(\theta_u^\ast\) (§4.1.6). A change of interpretation is therefore predicted only if exposure brings \(|\theta_u^\ast|\) down to where the criteria first hold, and whether any exposure does so is not tested. Predicate granularity should shift the lexical override threshold according to Eq. (4.6). The law is derived with the utility level severed, for \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,1)\) base world priors. Empirically, with \(\theta_u^\ast\) relearned, the measured slope is 36–45 per cent higher (§4.1.5).

The present architecture also predicts that the implicature of *some* is absent below a least number of atoms and present from this number upwards (§4.1.7). The architecture implements no mechanism that represents exhaustification, so the implicature in question holds independently of exhaustivity (§5.2.3). The least number is not a constant of the architecture: at \(\Lambda=512\) and \(Z=6\), the least number lies between 7 and 10 according to the base world prior, and under the four diffuse base world priors the least number does not rise as lexical strength falls. This paper offers two grounds on which a prediction of this kind may be desirable.

The first ground is that the scale \(\langle\textit{some},\textit{all}\rangle\) may presume more than two atoms. The sentence *John blinked all his eyes* is odd. The oddness can be understood as a contradiction between two presuppositions: the presupposition from world knowledge that a person has two eyes, and a presupposition of *all* that the scale has more than two atoms. The second ground is that the implicature appears unstable with few atoms. After the written sentences *John threw three marbles into the water. Some marbles sank*, in which the stress on *some* is not given, the inference that fewer than three marbles sank is not confidently available. By the author's introspective judgement, the implicature remains unstable at five atoms and first holds stably at six, where the listener holds no strong prior belief about the number of marbles that sank. Whether this judgement is general requires an empirical study.

The literature offers different treatments of both observations. The oddness of *all* with a domain of two is attributed to competition with *both*, rather than to a presupposition of *all*: a sentence with *all* requires that a domain of exactly two not be taken for granted, because the parallel sentence with *both* presupposes more (Percus, 2006, §§2–4; Sauerland, 2008, pp. 590–591, 595, for *every*), under a maxim to presuppose as much as possible (Heim, 1991, p. 515). Experiments also report an implicature in small domains. With three objects, all of which the speaker had looked at, participants bet less on three than on two (Goodman & Stuhlmüller, 2013, p. 179). With four marbles, the implicature was at least as strong as with sixteen, and the authors attribute the effect of the smaller set to the effort of establishing the size of a larger set (Degen & Goodman, 2014, pp. 398–400). *Some* is rated less natural for sets of one to three of thirteen objects, the more so when number terms are available, and with three objects a number term is the more natural description (Degen & Tanenhaus, 2015, §§1.4, 2.2 and 3.2).

This paper's position depends on the account of the partitive proposed in §5.2.3. The two experiments that report an implicature in small domains use the partitive *some of the* (§1.1). On this account, an entailed exhaustive reading is expected at any number of atoms above one, so these reports do not bear on a least number of atoms at which implicature holds independently of exhaustivity. The author's least number of six lies between the least numbers that the architecture gives under the default base world prior at \(\Lambda=128\) and \(\Lambda=512\), namely 5 and 7; lexical strength is not fitted to the judgement. For \(n\le4\) under this prior, the all-region mass rounds to 1.00 (§4.1.7), a stronger outcome than an unstable implicature. A study that varies the number of atoms from two upwards, with bare *some* in written sentences and without a stated base rate, would test the prediction.

The remaining predictions concern the proposed extension. Scales with interior cuts should partition log-odds space approximately uniformly, and *most* at \(\zeta=0\) is the first test of the proposed balanced search. This prediction rests on the conditional complexity argument of §5.2.4, which is not a derived bound. Under Conjecture I, predicates with unstable atomicity should require a finite \(\Lambda\), whereas predicates with stable atomicity, such as count nouns, place no upper bound on \(\Lambda\). The comparison of §4.2 does not test this prediction, because every adjective class falls on the unstable side and the maximum-class fit sets only a lower bound; this paper takes no position on the conjecture (§5.3.1). Conjectures II and IV concern an alternatives level that is not implemented, and Conjecture III relaxes a relation that the present architecture stipulates. None of the three is a prediction of the present architecture, and what each would predict depends on what remains to be derived (§5.4).

## 6.7 Conclusion

This paper asked what computational constraint scalar resolution might obey, whether resolution is a single feedforward pass or can be reached through recurrent interaction, and whether its patterns can arise as algorithmic-level peculiarities. The present architecture supplies conditional answers to these questions. Local computation and the free-energy objective structure the architecture: together they generate the local, error-driven inference through which an interpretation settles. The model would satisfy local plasticity at one utility dimension. At the two utility dimensions of the present architecture, however, the update of the single plastic parameter is not local, and this paper states this departure without repairing it (§3.4). The proposal of one opposition per level, with one utility dimension, would remove the departure if one column serves the pair as its cut moves, which remains to be derived (§§5.2.4 and 5.4). Scalar resolution in this architecture is jointly settled after the lexical entry is clamped. The situation and utility states reach one jointly determined optimum, without first forming a literal interpretation that is subsequently revised (§3.4). The model does not yet resolve how the lexical entry itself should be divided between semantics and pragmatics, because the entry is supplied rather than inferred (§3.3).

Scalar implicature does not require explicit within-trial competition in this implementation. Given the boundary conditions of §4.1.2, at \(Z=6\) and the stipulated \(n=10\), the settled belief under *some* has its mode outside the cell of *all* across the tested configurations in which the lexical entries hold against the base world prior (§4.1.7). Under the concentrated prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(64,1)\), the literal listener's mode lies inside the cell, settlement moves the mode out of the cell, and the normalized mass on the cell falls below one half (§4.1.4). The result is conditional on the number of atoms: at the settings of §4.1.4, the result fails below a least number of atoms, which lies between 7 and 10 according to the base world prior (§4.1.7). The effect arises by amplifying the observed entry's projection under a gain learned from earlier exposure, and the objective contains no unobserved utterance (§§4.1.4 and 5.2.1). Strengthening, by contrast, is not consistent. Where inference moves a mode or a mass majority out of the cell of *all*, the corresponding shift criterion holds (§4.1.5). However, at \(Z=6\), the utility level moves the settled mode downwards only from literal modes above approximately \(s=0.9405\), and upwards from modes below this location (§4.1.7). The two read-outs also disagree on shift under two of the five base world priors (§4.1.4).

The architecture predicts the extreme-favouring posterior of complete-scale adjectives from its scale structure. The literal listener moves towards the upper extreme relative to the elicited prior in both absolute classes and under both image types, as do the data and the full network, so the direction of this movement does not depend on settlement (§4.2.3). The orientation is also linked to the utility basis: at every \(n\) of the ladder, the maximum and minimum entries have identical tilt projections and differ in the sign of the even, width-sensitive projection (§4.2.5). The maximum class is consistent with a hard entry: both read-outs reach \(R^2=0.993\), and the data place only a lower bound on \(\Lambda\). The minimum class's full-network fit reaches 0.456, at the smallest \(n\) of the ladder and a finite \(\Lambda\) (§4.2.2). Under the linking assumptions of §4.2.6, Experiment 2's responses depart from the complementary truth regions that the architecture assigns to a maximum adjective and its minimum antonym. These results are conditional on the stipulations of §4.2.1. These results bear on the third question in a limited sense. The implicature, its dependence on the number of atoms and the pattern of shifts arise from the representations and dynamics of this architecture, and in this sense are algorithmic-level peculiarities of it (§6.4). Reproducing these patterns does not identify the architecture that produces them in the brain, since different representations and processes may predict the same patterns (§2.5).

The proposed alternatives level is a next-step specification. Four considerations motivate this level, on grounds of unequal strength: locality, derived at \(m=1\) for a fixed pair; a conditional complexity saving; one possible resolution of the asymmetry between antonyms; and the hypothesis that an explicit alternative could lower the mass-shift floor, although the level would not supply mode shift (§§5.2.4–5.2.5). Four conjectures accompany the level. Apart from the limited check of one prediction of Conjecture II against Experiment 2's individual responses, none is tested (§5.0). The algebra and the empirical consequences of the alternatives level remain to be developed (§5.4).

[^multidim]: A multidimensional adjective is one whose application depends on several dimensions at once, as *healthy* depends on blood pressure and cholesterol (Sassoon, 2013). If the dimensions are inferred jointly, the derivations carry over, but a grid over \(d\) scales costs \(K^d\) nodes, and the parametric representations that would avoid this cost cannot represent sharp lexical exclusions (nbtc 3 §9.3). A factored treatment, in which each dimension keeps its own scale and a quantifier binds the dimensions, as in Sassoon's typology, is not examined.
