# On the Nature of Disability

Flora Gu

---

There had long been debates on whether treatment of disability is in itself motivated by unjust discriminative beliefs. If we assume that no human is omnipotent, then we may assert that all humans are disabled because they are less than God. Apparently this is not what is usually meant when we call someone a person with disability. What are we referring to when we talk about disability? One might say that there are many aspects to being an abled person. Say one can reduce them to a single axis called the scale of ability, there is no upper bound to this scale. Then we must be referring to a threshold that lies somewhere on this scale, above which is ability and below which is disability.

To be less abstract, consider a reductio ad absurdum argument:

1. Some consider a blind person disabled because they are not able to see.
2. Some consider a person with schizophrenia disabled because they are not able to be sane all the time.
3. Some consider a person with autism disabled because they are not able to be adaptive.
4. Some consider a person with ADHD disabled because they are not able to focus.
5. Some consider a person who is homosexual disabled because they are not able to be straight.
6. Some consider a person who has small eyes disabled because they are not able to charm.
7. Some consider a dead person disabled because they are not able to live.

But why not consider a person disabled because they are not able to fly without the aid of external devices? It would be quite nice if a person could fly. An unsurprising response would be that normally a human is not able to fly, and that normally a human is able to see. Thus those who are lesser than the norm are disabled.

As such disability cannot be a natural property: surely such norms are not part of nature! Consider that most natural beings do not meet the normal level of intelligence, not to mention that plants are not even able to move around without the aid of external forces. I say that the norm is arbitrarily imposed; in other words, a disabled person is disabled by the society. That being said, there is however not much point to commenting on whether such norms are just or fair, since even if a norm is unfair or unjust, I suspect there is not much I can do in my lifetime to change others' minds. Committing to tasks that are likely unachievable would be frustrating.

When I told my friend once upon a time that I am determined to contribute to the development of devices that help disabled people own their own lives, my friend asked "What if they don't consider themselves less than abled or in need of help?" Indeed, for reasons explained above, I don't think anyone with different abilities than a stereotypical human should readily consider themselves less than abled. On the other hand, I find the question irrelevant to my determination, since whether such devices are available is a separate question from whether any given individual wants it or not. People who are stuck in this struggle should have the freedom to opt out of that struggle even if this end is only achieved via submission to an unpleasantly arbitrary norm.
